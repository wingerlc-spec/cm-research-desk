## 1. Who are the card lenders outside the top 100 banks, how big is the group, and how has its composition changed?

### Takeaway
The group holds only about 3% of commercial-bank card balances (my estimate from Fed data; third-party estimates say "roughly 2.5%" / "a few percent"), and its loss rates behave like a subprime-specialist portfolio, not like the average community bank. The gap did not open in 2022: it opened in a single quarter, Q3 2017, in a way that looks like a change in group membership, and has been 3 to 5 points ever since.

### Cited Findings
- Fed series, Q2 2026: card delinquency 6.49% small banks vs 2.74% large banks; charge-off rate 7.80% vs 3.70%; all commercial banks 2.85% delinquency and 3.82% charge-offs. The all-bank rate sits almost on top of the large-bank rate, which itself shows the small-bank group has little weight — local CSV; series pages [DRCCLOBS](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DRCCLOBS), [DRCCLT100S](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DRCCLT100S), [CORCCT100S](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CORCCT100S)
- History of the delinquency gap (small minus large, local CSV, same series): Q2 2005 4.15% vs 3.66% (+0.49); Q4 2005 2.94% vs 3.57% (small banks lower); Q2 2009 4.60% vs 6.87% (small banks 2.27 points LOWER at the crisis peak); Q4 2010 4.15% vs 4.14%; Q4 2015 2.80% vs 2.14%; Q2 2017 4.03% vs 2.46% (+1.57); Q3 2017 5.43% vs 2.49% (+2.94); Q1 2020 7.16% vs 2.55% (+4.61); Q4 2021 4.61% vs 1.47% (+3.14); Q4 2023 7.86% vs 2.96% (+4.90, series peak for small banks); Q2 2026 6.49% vs 2.74% (+3.75) — [DRCCLOBS](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DRCCLOBS)
- Charge-off rate at small banks jumped from 4.68% (Q2 2017) to 7.89% (Q3 2017) in one quarter while the large-bank rate went from 3.47% to 3.48%. Small-bank charge-off peak in the post-2000 data is 10.28% (Q4 2001); recent peak 9.53% (Q1 2024) — local CSV; [CORCCT100S](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CORCCT100S)
- Dollar net charge-offs on credit cards, small banks (millions, quarterly): Q2 2005 161; Q2 2017 239; Q3 2017 203; Q4 2017 210; Q4 2019 447; Q4 2023 639; Q1 2024 691; Q2 2025 658; Q1 2026 661; Q2 2026 637 — [FRED NCOALLCCOB](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/NCOALLCCOB)
- Dollar net charge-offs on credit cards, large banks (millions, quarterly): Q2 2005 3,208; Q2 2017 5,887; Q3 2017 5,625; Q4 2023 9,201; Q1 2025 11,201; Q2 2026 9,724 — [FRED NCOALLCCT100B](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/NCOALLCCT100B)
- A February 2026 independent analysis estimated non-top-100 banks hold about $27.6 billion of card balances, "roughly 2.5%" of the commercial-bank total, with the top 100 holding about 97% (about $937 billion). It names Credit One, First Premier and Merrick Bank as subprime specialists in the non-top-100 group, but states that individual issuers' rates were not verified. This is a derived estimate from a non-authoritative outlet — [plaintext.media, "The Ten-Cent Trap"](https://www.plaintext.media/article/20260215_210053_NonTop100_Banks_CreditCard_ChargeOffs_Ha)
- Another tracker describes the group as holding "a few percent" of commercial-bank card loans and states that the Fed gives no explanation for the gap and does not describe the borrowers behind either rate — [American Default, "The Other Banks"](https://american-default.pages.dev/indicators/the-other-banks/)
- How the Fed computes the rates: delinquency rate = dollar amount of delinquent loans (Call Report schedule RC-N) divided by total loans in the category (RC-C); charge-off rate = net charge-offs over the quarter divided by average loans, annualized (x400). Series are adjusted for mergers. The page fetched does not explain how or how often the top-100 list is re-ranked — [Federal Reserve, Charge-Off and Delinquency Rates, About](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/chargeoff/about.htm)
- Subprime exposure is not confined to small banks. A Bank Policy Institute study (31 May 2024, Q4 2023 balances, issuers with at least $1 billion of card balances) estimated subprime shares of each issuer's own balances at Capital One 32%, Synchrony 28%, Discover 20%, Bank of America 17%, Citi 16%, JPMorgan 14%; the six largest hold roughly 71% of subprime card balances (total subprime about $220 to $232 billion). Subprime specialists Merrick Bank, Stride Bank, United National and Credit One were treated as 100% subprime, each with under 2% of the subprime market; Comenity Capital Bank 59% subprime. Of 3,755 depository institutions with card balances, most fall below the $1 billion threshold. Note BPI is a large-bank trade group and wrote this in the context of the Capital One/Discover merger — [BPI](https://bpi.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Is-the-Subprime-Segment-of-the-Credit-Card-Market-Concentrated_.pdf)
- The typical small issuer is not a high-cost subprime lender. CFPB survey (156 issuers: 84 banks, 72 credit unions; first half 2023): median purchase APR for consumers with scores 619 or less was 28.49% at the top 25 issuers vs 20.62% at small issuers; 27% of large-issuer products carried an annual fee vs 9.5% at small issuers — [CFPB, Feb 2024](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/credit-card-data-small-issuers-offer-lower-rates/)
- Journalistic explanation from 2020: big banks win prime customers with rewards; smaller banks without those budgets issue high-rate, low-perk cards (25% to 30%+ APR) to subprime borrowers, so they "end up with more subprime customers". The article cites Q4 2019 small-bank delinquency of 7.05% vs 2.48% at the top 100 and about 4,500 banks in the small group; it names no lenders and gives no balance share — [Wolf Street, 22 Feb 2020](https://wolfstreet.com/2020/02/22/subprime-credit-card-delinquencies-spike-to-record-high-past-financial-crisis-peak-as-other-consumers-relish-the-good-times-why/)
- A December 2019 commentary noted the "huge surge" in small-bank card delinquency in Q3 2017 with no change at big banks, and reported that the St. Louis Fed said it did not know the cause — [Mother Jones / Kevin Drum, Dec 2019](https://fullsite.motherjones.com/kevin-drum/2019/12/why-are-small-bank-credit-card-delinquencies-up)

### Inferences
- Balance share (my calculation, approximate): dividing annualized quarterly net charge-off dollars by the published charge-off rate gives implied average card balances. Q2 2026: small banks about $33 billion (637 x 4 / 7.80%), large banks about $1,051 billion (9,724 x 4 / 3.70%), so small banks are about 3% of commercial-bank card loans. Q2 2005: about $12 billion vs about $303 billion, about 3.7%. Caveat: the rate is seasonally adjusted and the dollar series may not be, so these are rough; they are consistent with the "roughly 2.5%" and "a few percent" third-party figures above.
- The Q3 2017 break is a composition event, not a borrower-stress event. In that quarter small-bank charge-off dollars FELL (239 to 203 million) while the charge-off rate rose from 4.68% to 7.89%. That can only happen if the denominator shrank sharply: implied balances drop from about $20 billion to about $10 billion in one quarter. A large, low-loss card book appears to have left the group (by crossing into the top 100, merger, charter consolidation or portfolio sale), leaving a residual dominated by high-loss lenders. I did not find a source identifying the institution.
- Since the group is tiny and concentrated, a handful of charters can move the series. The pattern (charge-off rates of 8% to 9.5%, delinquency of 6% to 8%) matches subprime specialists, secured/"fee-harvester" card issuers and partner banks that book fintech card programs, rather than community banks whose own card pricing is lower than big banks' (CFPB). Which named charters currently sit outside the top 100 by assets was not verified.
- The headline framing "small-bank delinquency is above 2009" is misleading as a cycle comparison: in 2009 the small-bank group had LOWER card delinquency than large banks (4.60% vs 6.87% in Q2 2009). The series is not measuring the same kind of lender across time.
- The gap is therefore mostly a statement about lender mix. It does carry some borrower information: the 2021 to 2023 rise (4.19% in Q2 2021 to 7.86% in Q4 2023) and the decline since (6.49% in Q2 2026) track the same up-then-down path as subprime borrower delinquency in the score-band data in section 4.

### Gaps

- Not found: a current list of card-issuing banks outside the top 100 by assets with their card balances (would need Call Report/FFIEC data, not reachable here). ## 2. What have the Fed, regional Feds, FDIC or other researchers written to explain the small-bank surge since 2022?

### Takeaway
I found no official publication that directly explains the small-bank vs large-bank card gap; the Fed publishes the series without commentary. Official work explains the post-2022 rise in card delinquency generally: pandemic-era loosening of underwriting, score inflation, and inflation/rate pressure concentrated in nonprime and lower-income borrowers. Explanations of the gap itself come from the financial press and are assertions (borrower mix, no rewards budgets, fewer other income streams), not evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Fed Financial Stability Report (May 2026, data through Q4 2025): the rise in card delinquencies since early 2022 was "attributable primarily to elevated delinquencies among borrowers with nonprime credit scores"; card debt "shifted slightly to subprime borrowers over the past year", mainly from prime and near-prime borrowers migrating down; delinquency edged down in Q4 2025 but stayed high relative to the past 10 years; the stabilization is broad-based across score and income groups; the increase is linked to looser underwriting and strong real revolving-credit growth during the pandemic — [Federal Reserve FSR, May 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-may-financial-stability-report-borrowing.htm)
- New York Fed (10 Nov 2026): new card delinquency rose sharply between 2022 and 2024, steepest in the lowest-income ZIP quartile; the gap between high- and low-income ZIPs widened after 2022; rates are above pre-pandemic for all income groups. The share of new cards going to the lowest-income quartile rose nearly 2 percentage points at the start of the pandemic and stayed elevated until 2023; median score at card issuance fell from 699 (2017 to 2019 average) to 688 (2020 to 2022) and has since moved above the pre-pandemic level. The 2020 to 2022 vintage peaked high through 2023 into 2024. Authors judge macro conditions (inflation outpacing low-income wage growth, higher rates, exhausted savings) the main driver and looser credit supply secondary, and expect improvement to continue absent shocks — [Liberty Street Economics](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/11/a-look-at-the-wider-divide-in-credit-card-delinquency-by-income/)
- Related official work located but not read in full: FEDS Note "The Effects of Credit Score Migration on Subprime Auto Loan and Credit Card Delinquencies" (12 Jan 2024) — [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/the-effects-of-credit-score-migration-on-subprime-auto-loan-and-credit-card-delinquencies-20240112.html); FEDS Note "Predicting Credit Card Delinquency Rates" (28 Feb 2025) — [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/predicting-credit-card-delinquency-rates-20250228.html); Boston Fed "Are Recently Issued Credit Card Accounts More Risky?" (2025) — [Boston Fed](https://www.bostonfed.org/publications/current-policy-perspectives/2025/are-recently-issued-credit-card-accounts-risky.aspx). Titles only; contents not verified.
- Press explanation (Motley Fool, 2 Sep 2026): large banks have investment-banking, corporate and trading income so can keep card standards tight; smaller banks lack those streams and take more credit risk with lower-score customers. No data offered in support — [AOL / Motley Fool](https://www.aol.com/articles/credit-card-delinquencies-run-6-055000000.html)
- Press explanation (Aug 2026): small-bank rate of about 6.4% reflects "smaller, subprime-heavy banks" and "real stress concentrated among the most vulnerable borrowers"; no named banks or balance share — [Mavengity](https://mavengity.com/articles/finance/why-credit-card-delinquencies-look-worse-than-they-are/)
- Proposed (unconfirmed by its author) rate mechanism: variable card APRs tied to prime raised minimum payments when the policy rate reached 5.33% in mid-2023, tipping stretched borrowers into default; the author notes Bread Financial's 7.4% net charge-off rate sits below the non-top-100 average — [plaintext.media](https://www.plaintext.media/article/20260215_210053_NonTop100_Banks_CreditCard_ChargeOffs_Ha)

### Inferences
- The official explanations (nonprime concentration, loose 2020 to 2022 vintages, low-income squeeze) are consistent with a small-bank group whose book is mostly nonprime: such a book amplifies exactly the borrower segment that deteriorated most. But the level of the gap (3 to 5 points since 2017) predates 2022 and is structural.
- The Motley Fool "other income streams" story does not fit the evidence: the BPI data show the biggest issuers hold about 71% of subprime balances, and the CFPB data show ordinary small issuers price below big banks. The gap reflects a few specialists, not small banks as a class.

### Gaps
- Not found: St. Louis Fed On the Economy, Philadelphia Fed, FDIC Quarterly Banking Profile or CFPB card market report text addressing the small-bank gap specifically. The FDIC QBP and the CFPB biennial card report were not retrieved in this session.
- The three Fed/Boston Fed papers listed by title were not read; their findings are not found here.

## 3. Philadelphia Fed Large Bank Credit Card and Mortgage Data: latest edition

### Takeaway
At large banks, the latest edition (Q2 2026 data, published 7 Oct 2026) shows improvement, not deterioration: past-due shares at multi-year lows, full-payers at a series-matching high of 37%, and utilization (Q1 2026) the lowest in three years. Minimum-payment share remains above 2019 levels and only slightly below its Q4 2024 record. The report's narrative does not publish delinquency by score band; the score-band story it tells is on originations, where sub-660 lending is rising again.

### Cited Findings
- Edition: Q2 2026 narrative by Neil Bhutta and Anna Veksler, published 7 Oct 2026. ### Inferences
- Large-bank 30+ day delinquency in Q2 2026 (2.99%) is about 0.4 points above Q2 2019 (2.61%) and below the Q3 2012 starting value (3.18%); the large-bank picture is normalization after a 2024 peak.

That is a leading indicator to watch, since the 2020 to 2022 loosening is what the NY Fed blames for the 2023 to 2024 peak.

## 4. What do credit-score-band and income-band breakdowns show? Is subprime/near-prime at or above 2008 to 2010 levels?

### Takeaway
No. In the Fed's own score-band data (through Q3 2025), subprime card delinquency was 16.28% against a 2009 peak of 23.08%, near-prime 5.67% vs 9.32%, prime 1.28% vs 2.36%. All tiers are above their early-2020 levels and stress is concentrated in nonprime and low-income borrowers, but nothing is at crisis levels. 2026 data show stable-to-improving balance-weighted delinquency with a mild rise in the borrower-level rate and a slow drift of consumers into lower tiers.

### Cited Findings
- FEDS Note data (dated 24 Nov 2025 per URL; accessible-data page read, narrative not): card delinquency all borrowers 4.03% in Q3 2025, down from 4.25% in Q3 2024; 2009 Q2 peak 7.17%; Q3 2019 3.20%; Q3 2021 low 1.92%. By score (Q3 2025 / 2009 peak / Q1 2020): subprime (<620) 16.28% / 23.08% / 13.62%; near-prime (620 to 719) 5.67% / 9.32% / 4.32%; prime (>719) 1.28% / 2.36% / 0.89%. Share of borrowers: subprime 20.01% in Q3 2025, up from a low of 17.64% in Q1 2022 (27.97% in Q1 2003); near-prime 23.07%; prime 56.92%. By census-tract income, Q4 2019: low-income 6.04% vs high-income 2.29% (later values not captured) — [Federal Reserve FEDS Note, accessible data](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/a-note-on-recent-dynamics-of-consumer-delinquency-rates-accessible-20251124.htm)
- Fed FSR May 2026 (data through Q4 2025): rise since early 2022 primarily from nonprime borrowers; card debt shifting slightly toward subprime through downward score migration; stabilization broad-based. Internal inconsistency in the source: text says delinquency is "elevated relative to the past 10 years" while a chart title says it "leveled off at their long-term median" — [Federal Reserve FSR](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-may-financial-stability-report-borrowing.htm)
- TransUnion Q3 2026 Credit Industry Insights Report (released 5 Nov 2026): bankcard balance-level 90+ DPD 1.99% (Q3 2025 2.05%; Q3 2024 2.17%; Q3 2023 1.91%); borrower-level 90+ DPD 2.50% (Q3 2025 2.37%; Q3 2024 2.43%; Q3 2023 2.34%); balances $1.16 trillion (+4.5% year over year); below-prime share of originations 42.3% (40.6% a year earlier); originations +9.5% year over year with subprime +16.5% and near-prime +11.6%; charge-offs about flat ($15.79 billion to $15.76 billion). Tier-level delinquency rates were not in the release summary read — [TransUnion via Finviz](https://finviz.com/news/416965/credit-access-expands-as-lenders-pair-growth-with-more-strategic-risk-management)
- VantageScore CreditGauge, September 2026 (published 26 Oct 2026; all products, not card-specific): 30 to 59 DPD 1.15%, 2 bps above September 2025; 90 to 119 DPD 0.23%, 1 bp above a year earlier; average VantageScore 4.0 701, steady; tier shares over two years: prime 33.3% to 32.5%, near-prime 17.4% to 18.0%, subprime 18.4% to 18.7% — [VantageScore](https://vantagescore.com/resources/knowledge-center/press_releases/vantagescore-creditgauge-september-2026-overall-balances-hold-steady-as-consumer-credit-remains-stable)
- New York Fed (Q3 2026): flow into serious (90+ day) delinquency for all debt 2.64% annualized in Q3 2026 vs 3.03% in Q3 2025 (local CSV NYFED_HHDC_2026Q3, sourced to the NY Fed release) — [New York Fed press release, 10 Nov 2026](https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2026/20261110)
- New York Fed by ZIP income (10 Nov 2026): steepest rise in new card delinquency 2022 to 2024 was in the lowest-income quartile, gap widened, all quartiles above pre-pandemic; exact quartile rates are only in charts — [Liberty Street Economics](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/11/a-look-at-the-wider-divide-in-credit-card-delinquency-by-income/)
- Richmond Fed (Feb 2026) summarizing a Fed study: card delinquency flattened through Q3 2025 across score tiers, income groups and homeownership status; "no clear evidence of elevated consumer financial stress through 2025" — [Richmond Fed EB 26-05](https://richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_brief/2026/eb_26-05)

### Inferences
- The premise that subprime/near-prime card delinquency is "at or above 2008 to 2010 levels" is not supported: as of Q3 2025 each tier was roughly 60% to 70% of its 2009 peak (16.28 vs 23.08; 5.67 vs 9.32; 1.28 vs 2.36) and about 20% to 45% above Q1 2020. Prime is low in absolute terms but its relative rise over Q1 2020 (0.89% to 1.28%) is as large proportionally as subprime's.
- The aggregate rate rose partly through mix: the subprime borrower share went from 17.64% (Q1 2022) to 20.01% (Q3 2025), and VantageScore still shows slow migration from prime to near-prime/subprime through September 2026. Composition matters at the borrower level as well as the lender level.
- TransUnion's Q3 2026 divergence (balance-level 90+ DPD down to 1.99%, borrower-level up to 2.50%) implies more delinquent borrowers with smaller balances, consistent with stress at the low-limit, low-score end and with rapid subprime origination growth (+16.5%).
- Taken together: the small-bank series is a magnified view of the nonprime segment. It is useful as a direction indicator for that segment (improving since Q4 2023, with Q2 2026 delinquency ticking up 5 bps to 6.49% from 6.44%), not as a level comparison with 2009 or with large banks.

### Gaps

- Not found: New York Fed card delinquency by age for 2026, or numeric ZIP-income quartile rates (charts only).
- Not found: VantageScore October 2026 CreditGauge, or card-specific tier delinquency from VantageScore.

## 5. Are fintech and BNPL lenders showing similar stress, and does BNPL "phantom debt" hide stress that card data would miss?

### Takeaway
Fintech personal lending shows the same split as cards: balance-weighted delinquency falling but borrower-level delinquency rising sharply amid record, mostly below-prime originations. BNPL is real but small: about $3 billion outstanding at a time versus $1.2 trillion of card debt, so it cannot hide aggregate stress, though it is concentrated in exactly the deep-subprime users whose stress the small-bank series picks up, and most of it is still unreported to bureaus.

### Cited Findings
- Unsecured personal loans, TransUnion Q3 2026: balance-level 60+ DPD 1.95% (2.01% in Q3 2025; 2.17% in Q3 2023) but borrower-level 60+ DPD 4.12% (3.52% in Q3 2025; 3.75% in Q3 2023); originations a record 8.41 million (+22.7% year over year); below-prime share of originations 69.0% (66.1% a year earlier; 63.0% in Q3 2023); fintechs originated 49.4% of all personal loans and drove most of the growth; balances $293 billion (+9.0%); charge-offs down 11% to $3.5 billion — [TransUnion via Finviz](https://finviz.com/news/416965/credit-access-expands-as-lenders-pair-growth-with-more-strategic-risk-management)
- BNPL size: real transaction value grew roughly 20% a year after 2021 to an estimated $70 billion in 2025, about 1.1% of card purchase volume (over $6.3 trillion); average outstanding BNPL debt about $3.02 billion, roughly 400 times smaller than the $1.23 trillion of card debt at end Q3 2025 — [Richmond Fed EB 26-05, Zhu Wang, Feb 2026](https://richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_brief/2026/eb_26-05)
- BNPL users: 21% of consumers with a credit record used one of the six largest providers in 2022 (CFPB); deep subprime (FICO 300 to 579) were 45% of originations in 2021 to 2022 and subprime (580 to 619) another 16% — [Richmond Fed EB 26-05](https://richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_brief/2026/eb_26-05)
- BNPL losses: charge-off rate 2.63% in 2022 and 1.83% in 2023, vs 4.19% for bank cards in Q4 2023; even no-score and deep-subprime users repaid 96% of BNPL loans. LendingTree 2025 survey: 41% of users paid late at least once in the past year, up from 34%; no direct evidence of rising aggregate BNPL charge-offs — [Richmond Fed EB 26-05](https://richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_brief/2026/eb_26-05)
- Reporting: pay-in-four loans generally involve no hard inquiry and are generally not reported to bureaus; Affirm began reporting in 2025, Klarna and Afterpay have resisted, Sezzle is opt-in. BNPL users carry higher balances on cards and other unsecured credit than non-users, with causation unclear; the brief finds no material BNPL-driven rise in household indebtedness — [Richmond Fed EB 26-05](https://richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_brief/2026/eb_26-05)
- CFPB (December 2025 market report, as relayed by a secondary site): six large BNPL lenders made 335.8 million loans totaling $45.2 billion in 2023 (inflation-adjusted; $43.9 billion nominal), a yearly flow, not a balance — [American Default, "Phantom Debt"](https://american-default.pages.dev/analysis/phantom-debt/)

### Inferences
- "Phantom debt" is a measurement gap about who is stretched, not a hidden mass of debt: $3 billion outstanding is well under 1% of card balances, and smaller than my rough estimate of the small-bank card book itself (about $33 billion). It cannot explain or offset the small-bank gap.
- Where it matters is at the margin: 61% of BNPL originations (2021 to 2022) went to borrowers under FICO 620, the same population that dominates subprime-specialist card books. Late-payment prevalence among users rising to 41% is a soft signal consistent with nonprime strain, though survey-based.
- The fintech personal-loan pattern (borrower-level delinquency +60 bps year over year with record below-prime originations) is the clearest 2026 sign that risk is being added at the low end again, mirroring the sub-660 card origination pickup at large banks. Balance-weighted measures are being flattered by fast growth in the denominator.
