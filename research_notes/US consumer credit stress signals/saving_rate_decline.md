# US personal saving rate decline and when it is a warning sign (as of November 17, 2026)

Notes on conventions: "desk calc" means a figure computed in this session from the FRED CSVs already collected in the workspace (PSAVERT, PCE, PCEC96, DSPI, TDSP, CDSP, MDSP, TNWBSHNO; vintages dated Sept 11 to Oct 29, 2026) and is cited to the FRED series page. Everything else was read from the linked source in this session. Several press sources quote pre-revision vintages of the saving rate; these are flagged because the September 2026 annual update moved the series a lot.

## 1. What is driving the 2025 to 2026 decline?

### Takeaway
The decline is mainly a price shock that households chose to spend through: an oil and gasoline shock that began with the Iran war in late February 2026 pushed PCE inflation to 3.6% while nominal income growth stayed near 4.6%, so real disposable income is up only about 1% year over year against real spending growth of about 2.5%. Record net worth (wealth effect) and a still-low layoff rate explain why households kept spending; a spring tax-refund boost has faded; higher interest costs and depleted pandemic savings are background factors, not the 2026 trigger.

Almost all of the drop came after February 2026, i.e. after the war began — [FRED PSAVERT](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PSAVERT)

- Desk calc: net worth is about 8.2x annualized DPI in Q2 2026, versus about 7.7x in Q2 2025 and about 7.0x in Q4 2019 — [FRED TNWBSHNO](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/TNWBSHNO); [FRED DSPI](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DSPI)

- The boost to deposits from the 2026 tax refund season "appears to have run its course" (as of October 2026 data) — [BofA Institute Consumer Checkpoint, Nov 2026 (PDF)](https://institute.bankofamerica.com/content/dam/economic-insights/consumer-checkpoint-november-2026.pdf)
- The saving rate rose to 5.6% in Jan 2026 and 5.3% in Feb 2026 before falling, consistent with a refund-season income bump — [FRED PSAVERT](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PSAVERT)

Interest costs
- Household debt service ratio 11.11% of DPI in Q2 2026, versus 11.12% in Q2 2025, 11.32% in Q4 2025 (recent peak) and 11.73% in Q4 2019. Debt service has not risen over the year in which the saving rate fell — [FRED TDSP](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/TDSP)
- The Fed raised rates by a quarter point at its Sept 15 to 16, 2026 meeting, to 3.75% to 4.00%, and the 10-year Treasury yield was 5.042% on Oct 28, 2026, so interest costs are a forward risk — [News Tribune (AP), Oct 29, 2026](https://www.newstribune.com/news/2026/oct/29/fed-leaves-rates-unchanged-as-it-awaits/)

Pandemic excess savings
- SF Fed (DFA-based, real terms): the bottom 80% by income had spent all extra pandemic liquid wealth by late 2021, the top 20% by the second half of 2022; by Q1 2024 real liquid assets were 13% below the pre-pandemic projection for the bottom 80% and 2% below for the top 20% — [SF Fed Economic Letter 2024-21](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/el2024-21.pdf)

### Inferences
- A rough decomposition of the year to Sept 2026: nominal spending outran nominal income by about 1.7 pt (6.3% versus 4.6%), which accounts for the 1.3 pt fall in the saving rate. Because inflation ran 3.6%, real income grew only about 1.0% while real spending grew 2.5%; households held real consumption roughly on trend and let saving absorb the price shock. This is the 2022 pattern (inflation shock, saving rate to 2.4%) in smaller form.
- Energy is the largest identifiable driver: gasoline alone adds over 1 pt to nominal card spending growth, and the timing (saving rate 5.3% in Feb, 4.4% by April) lines up with the war. Tariffs were not mentioned as a 2026 driver in any source read this session.
- Interest payments are not a 2025 to 2026 driver in the aggregate DSR data, but the September 2026 hike and market pricing for more make them a 2027 risk.
- Pandemic excess savings were exhausted years ago on the SF Fed measure, so they cannot explain a 2026 decline; the buffer being used now is ordinary flow saving plus asset wealth.

## 2. How is saving distributed by income and wealth group?

### Takeaway
There is no official saving rate by income group, and the proxies conflict: bank-internal data (BofA, JPMorgan Chase Institute) say deposits remain above 2019 in real terms for all income cohorts, while the Fed's DFA-based work says the bottom 80% ran below their pre-pandemic trend. The claim that lower-income liquid balances are below 2019 levels in real terms is not supported by what was found; what is supported is that they are well below their 2021 peak and below trend, while wealth gains are concentrated at the top.

### Cited Findings
- BofA (October 2026 data): deposits are "still above pre-pandemic inflation-adjusted levels across all income cohorts"; household finances "broadly healthy"; a higher share of cardholders paying balances in full; but "a small rise in minimum-payment holders and a slight increase in 401(k) hardship distributions in Q3 2026" — [BofA Institute Consumer Checkpoint, Nov 2026 (PDF)](https://institute.bankofamerica.com/content/dam/economic-insights/consumer-checkpoint-november-2026.pdf)
- BofA (October 2026): card spending growth by income: lower-income +5.9% y/y (strongest), top 5% +5.7%, middle and higher around 5%; after-tax wage growth lower-income 4.7%, middle "just below 4%", higher 3.4%; the discretionary spending gap between top 5% and lower-income is "at its smallest since May 2025" — [BofA Institute Consumer Checkpoint, Nov 2026 (PDF)](https://institute.bankofamerica.com/content/dam/economic-insights/consumer-checkpoint-november-2026.pdf)
- BofA (September 2026): BNPL usage rising "but not at a rate that is overly concerning"; "nearly one in every five households that use BNPL has a repayment of over $500" — [BofA Institute Consumer Checkpoint, Oct 2026](https://institute.bankofamerica.com/economic-insights/consumer-checkpoint-october-2026.html)
- JPMorgan Chase Institute (data through October 2025, published Nov 25, 2025; latest edition found): median real checking plus savings balances "up 23 percent relative to pre-pandemic levels in 2019" but flat since early 2024; at the pre-pandemic growth rate of about 6% a year they would be up about 40%. Low-income real balances were +1% y/y in Oct 2025; high-income were -2% y/y, possibly reflecting transfers to higher-yield accounts. Median real income growth for prime-age workers was 1.6% — [JPMorgan Chase Institute](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/all-topics/household-financial-health/real-income-sustains-weak-trend-cash-liquidity-remains-stable)
- JPMCI: younger and lower-income people may have "just enough to spend, but not enough to splurge"; high-income households were about twice as likely as low-income to be investors from 2020 onward — [JPMorgan Chase Institute](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/all-topics/household-financial-health/real-income-sustains-weak-trend-cash-liquidity-remains-stable)
- SF Fed (DFA, real, through Q1 2024): bottom 80% by income 13% below pre-pandemic projection of liquid assets; top 20% 2% below. Top 20% money market holdings were 32% above the no-pandemic path versus 12% for the bottom 80%. Bottom-80% credit card delinquencies exceeded pre-pandemic levels around Q4 2021, the same quarter the extra liquid wealth ran out; the top 20% followed about four quarters later — [SF Fed Economic Letter 2024-21](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/el2024-21.pdf)
- DFA, bottom 50% by wealth, total deposits (nominal, $ millions): 534,844 (Q4 2019); 790,809 (Q4 2021); 779,678 (Q4 2022); 742,773 (Q4 2023); 741,686 (Q4 2024); 765,247 (Q4 2025); 785,612 (Q1 2026); 777,430 (Q2 2026) — [FRED WFRBLDEB50](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/WFRBLDEB50)
- Desk calc on that series using the PCE deflator (up about 25.9% from Q4 2019 to Q2 2026): bottom-50% deposits are up about 45% nominal and about 15% real versus Q4 2019, but about 16% below Q4 2021 in real terms — [FRED WFRBLDEB50](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/WFRBLDEB50); [FRED PCE](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PCE)
- DFA wealth shares, Q2 2026 (released Sept 18, 2026): top 1% 32.5% of net worth (highest since the series began in 1989, up 1.4 pt over the past year); next 9% 36.4%; next 40% 28.8%; bottom 50% 2.3%. Top 1% hold 50.9% of corporate equities and mutual fund shares — [24/7 Wall St, Nov 14, 2026](https://247wallst.com/personal-finance/2026/11/14/the-top-1-now-hold-32-5-of-u-s-household-wealth-the-highest-share-in-fed-records-going-back-to-1989-the-bottom-half-holds-2-3/)

### Inferences
- "Below 2019 in real terms" is the wrong test for lower-income households on the data found; the defensible statement is "above 2019 in level, below pre-pandemic trend, and far below the 2021 peak." The SF Fed measures against a rising trend; BofA and JPMCI measure against the 2019 level, which explains most of the apparent conflict.
- The $20.6tn net worth gain in the year to Q2 2026 accrued disproportionately to the top 10% (69% of net worth; top 1% holds half of directly held equities), so the wealth-effect support for spending is concentrated among high-income households. Lower-income spending is being supported by wage growth (4.7%) that is barely above 3.6% PCE inflation, not by balance sheets.
- Bank-customer panels exclude the unbanked and may skew toward more stable households; early stress markers (minimum payments, 401(k) hardship withdrawals) are moving in the wrong direction from a low base.

## 3. How reliable is the saving rate?

### Takeaway
Not very, at first print: it is a small residual between two very large numbers and is typically revised upward, mainly through income. The September 2026 annual update is a fresh example: April 2026 was first reported at 2.6% and July 2026 at 3.0%, and now stand at 4.4% and 4.5%. The level is therefore unreliable in real time; the direction over several months has been more robust.

### Cited Findings
- Real-time versus revised, 2026: the saving rate was reported at 2.6% for April 2026 ("lowest reading since June 2022") in the May 2026 release — [Econostrum via Newswav, May 29, 2026](https://newswav.com/article/americans-are-running-out-of-cushion-savings-rates-fall-to-2022-lows-A2605_MbFujU); the current vintage shows 4.4% for April 2026 — [FRED PSAVERT](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PSAVERT)
- July 2026 was first reported at 3.0% and revised to about 4.6% in the annual update released with the August 2026 data — [Voice for Liberty](https://www.wichitaliberty.org/?p=41196); it now stands at 4.5% — [FRED PSAVERT](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PSAVERT)
- November 2025 was reported at 3.5% as of January 2026 ("lowest in about three years"; November 2024 then shown as 4.9%) — [Fisher Investments, Jan 30, 2026](https://www.fisherinvestments.com/en-us/insights/market-commentary/digging-into-americas-declining-savings-rate); the current vintage shows 4.9% for November 2025 — [FRED PSAVERT](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PSAVERT)
- The 2026 annual update revised personal income and outlays back to January 2021, incorporating Q1 2026 QCEW wage data, revised CES payroll data for April to July 2026, and updated Medicaid benefits data — [BEA Personal Income and Outlays, August 2026](https://bea.gov/news/2026/personal-income-and-outlays-august-2026)
- KPMG on the 2026 update: compensation, including employer-paid benefits, had the largest upward revisions; better tax, business and government records found household resources earlier estimates missed — [KPMG, August 2026 PCE](https://kpmg.com/us/en/articles/2026/august-2026-pce.html)
- The 2026 update also changed price measurement (portfolio management fees, software, legal services) back to 2021; RBC estimated it trims core PCE inflation by about 0.18 pt; July 2026 headline PCE y/y went from 3.7% to 3.4% and core from 3.3% to 3.0%. RBC called the change "not a disinflationary signal" — [Voice for Liberty](https://www.wichitaliberty.org/?p=41196)
- 2024 annual update: personal income revised up $248.1bn (1.1%) for 2022 and $441.2bn (1.9%) for 2023; annual saving rate revised 2019 7.4% to 7.3%, 2020 15.4% to 15.3%, 2021 11.4% to 11.2%, 2022 3.3% to 3.0%, 2023 4.5% to 4.7% — [BEA, 2024 Annual Update Summary of Results](https://www.bea.gov/sites/default/files/2024-09/NEA-AU24-Summary-of-Results.pdf)
- June 2022 low: quoted as 2.2% in a January 2026 article — [Fisher Investments](https://www.fisherinvestments.com/en-us/insights/market-commentary/digging-into-americas-declining-savings-rate); 2.4% in the current vintage — [FRED PSAVERT](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PSAVERT)
- 2005 was originally published as a negative saving rate: "-0.4 percent during 2005" — [St. Louis Fed Regional Economist, July 2006](https://www.stlouisfed.org/publications/regional-economist/july-2006/as-household-asset-values-rise-should-we-still-worry-about-the-saving-rate); and -1.1% for 2006 — [NY Fed Current Issues 13-4](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/current_issues/ci13-4.pdf). Yet in the latest data analysed by Croushore and co-author, "the smallest personal saving rate is 2.4 percent" over their sample, even though the initial release "showed a negative personal saving rate" in a number of quarters — [Croushore et al., "PSR" working paper, Apr 2022](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~dcrousho/docs/PSR_2022Apr21.pdf)
- Same paper: the saving rate is "generally revised upwards"; revisions are mainly upward revisions to income (noise-to-signal ratio 5.87 for income versus 0.53 for outlays and 2.19 for the saving rate); the 1999 and 2013 benchmarks had particularly large effects; correcting the initial release for past average bias cuts forecast error by about 17% to 19%. Conclusion: "Any alarm about a low rate of personal saving is likely misplaced" and policymakers "may place too much emphasis on the signal coming from recent data" — [Croushore et al.](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~dcrousho/docs/PSR_2022Apr21.pdf)
- NY Fed: early readings for the mid and late 1970s showed low saving that was later revised to fairly high; saving is a residual (DPI less outlays); share repurchases are excluded from income while dividends are included (net repurchases rose from $42bn in 2003 to $602bn in 2006); capital gains are excluded but taxes on them are deducted — [NY Fed Current Issues 13-4](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/current_issues/ci13-4.pdf)
- Other definitional omissions: retirement account withdrawals and pension payouts are not income; debt principal repayment counts as saving — [Fisher Investments](https://www.fisherinvestments.com/en-us/insights/market-commentary/digging-into-americas-declining-savings-rate)

### Inferences
- In 2026 the first-print error was 1.4 to 1.8 pt on a rate of 3% to 4.5% (April: 2.6% first, 4.4% now; July: 3.0% first, 4.5% now; November 2025: 3.5% then, 4.9% now). A threshold-style rule such as "below 3% is a warning" would have fired in spring 2026 and then been revised away.
- The current 3.8% for September 2026 is post-annual-update but still rests on preliminary 2026 source data; the historical tendency is for later upward revision. Not every update is upward, though: the 2024 update lowered 2022 from 3.3% to 3.0%.
- The decline itself survived revision: both the pre-update and post-update vintages show a falling rate through 2026, and the companion data (real DPI growth of about 1% versus real PCE growth of 2.5%) are independent of the residual's level.
- Asset booms and buyback-heavy markets bias the measured rate down, which matters in 2026 given record net worth.

## 4. What happened historically after the saving rate fell to low levels?

### Takeaway
A low saving rate on its own has been a poor timing signal: lows in the late 1990s, 2005 and 2022 were followed by years of continued spending or no recession at all. It mattered when an asset-price and labor-market shock hit households whose spending depended on wealth or credit (2001, 2008); the saving rate then rose as a reaction, not a lead.

### Cited Findings
- 2005 to 2007: the saving rate (as then measured) hit 1.4% in July 2005, more than two years before the recession began in December 2007; personal consumption kept growing until the second half of 2008; PCE then fell from Q3 2008 to Q2 2009, with the sharpest drop -3.5% annualized in Q4 2008 — [Fisher Investments, Jan 30, 2026](https://www.fisherinvestments.com/en-us/insights/market-commentary/digging-into-americas-declining-savings-rate)
- 2021 to 2022: the rate fell from 6.6% (Dec 2021) to 2.2% (June 2022, vintage at the time) and no recession followed; "the savings rate tends to rise during recessions," reflecting reactions to conditions and not predicting them — [Fisher Investments](https://www.fisherinvestments.com/en-us/insights/market-commentary/digging-into-americas-declining-savings-rate)
- Current vintage: the rate was below 4% from March through October 2022 (low 2.4% in June 2022), recovered to 4.4% by December 2022, and September 2026 is the first sub-4% month since — [FRED PSAVERT](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PSAVERT)
- June 2022 context: CPI inflation peaked at 9.1% and gasoline hit $5 a gallon — [Econostrum via Newswav](https://newswav.com/article/americans-are-running-out-of-cushion-savings-rates-fall-to-2022-lows-A2605_MbFujU)
- Late 1990s to mid 2000s averages as then published: 8.6% for 1950 to 1992, 4.6% for 1993 to 1998, 2.2% for 1999 to 2004, -0.4% in 2005, -1.1% in 2006 — [NY Fed Current Issues 13-4](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/current_issues/ci13-4.pdf)
- NY Fed regression test: the saving rate had little predictive power for spending growth; a 1 pt drop in the saving rate was associated with about a 0.024 pt drop in real spending growth. Household energy spending rose from 4.6% of after-tax income in 2003 to 5.8% in 2006, one reason saving fell then — [NY Fed Current Issues 13-4](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/current_issues/ci13-4.pdf)
- Wealth backdrop in 2005: household net assets rose almost $3.9tn in 2005, almost 42% of DPI; the St. Louis Fed author nonetheless argued low saving remained a long-run concern because asset gains overstate real resources — [St. Louis Fed Regional Economist, July 2006](https://www.stlouisfed.org/publications/regional-economist/july-2006/as-household-asset-values-rise-should-we-still-worry-about-the-saving-rate)
- Debt service at the 2007 peak versus now: household DSR 15.85% of DPI in Q4 2007 versus 11.11% in Q2 2026; mortgage DSR 8.95% versus 5.83% — [FRED TDSP](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/TDSP); [FRED MDSP](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MDSP)
- Liquidity link to credit stress: bottom-80% card delinquencies crossed above pre-pandemic levels in the quarter their extra liquid wealth ran out (Q4 2021) — [SF Fed Economic Letter 2024-21](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/el2024-21.pdf)
- 2026 warning from a bank economist: oil at $85 to $100 for several months could "materially increase the risk of recession" (Luke Tilley, Wilmington Trust); BNY's Vincent Reinhart: "We're not at the big enough stage" (both March 2026) — [The Daily Record (AP), Mar 10, 2026](https://thedailyrecord.com/2026/03/10/rising-gas-prices-stock-volatility-us-consumers-iran-conflict/)

### Inferences
- Pattern across episodes: (a) late 1990s and 2005 to 2007, low saving driven by wealth gains, ended by an asset-price collapse and job losses, with a two-plus-year lag in the 2000s; (b) 2022, low saving driven by an inflation and energy shock, resolved without recession because employment and nominal incomes held and inflation fell. 2026 has features of both: an energy shock like 2022 and record wealth like the 2000s, but with a much lower debt service burden than 2007.
- The 2022 episode had a pandemic liquidity cushion that the SF Fed says was gone for the bottom 80% by late 2021 and for the top 20% by late 2022; 2026 lacks that buffer, so the same saving rate implies less resilience among lower-income households.
- A low saving rate is better read as a measure of vulnerability (how little slack there is if incomes or asset prices fall) than as a forecast. It was a credit-stress signal for lower-income borrowers when paired with depleted liquid assets, per the SF Fed delinquency timing.

### Gaps
- A formal study testing the saving rate as a recession predictor (hit rate, false positives): not found beyond the NY Fed regression and practitioner commentary. The Fisher Investments piece is an asset manager's commentary, not research.
- Late-1990s episode detail (what happened to spending after the 2000 equity decline): not found in sources read this session.

## 5. Thresholds and companion indicators; household balance sheet backdrop now

### Takeaway
No source read this session offers a formal saving-rate threshold; the workable approach is to pair it with labor-market, real-income, liquidity and debt-service indicators. On those companions the picture in November 2026 is mixed: unemployment is low and stable, net worth is at a record and debt service is below 2019, but real wages are falling, real income growth is about 1%, and early-stage stress markers are rising.

### Cited Findings
- Informal threshold cited in press: the saving rate "has dipped below 3% only during extreme financial stress, such as June 2022 and briefly before the 2008 financial crisis" (statement made on pre-revision data) — [Econostrum via Newswav, May 29, 2026](https://newswav.com/article/americans-are-running-out-of-cushion-savings-rates-fall-to-2022-lows-A2605_MbFujU)
- Spending versus income gap: nominal PCE growth exceeded DPI growth by about 1.7 pt over the year to Sept 2026 (6.3% versus 4.6%); real PCE +2.5% versus real DPI +1.0% (desk calc). The gap opened after Feb 2026, i.e. about seven months so far — [FRED PCE](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PCE); [FRED DSPI](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DSPI)
- Labor market: October 2026 payrolls +165,000, unemployment 4.1%; 2026 year-to-date average 68,000 jobs a month; layoffs low, "low-fire, low-hire" environment; Yale Budget Lab puts break-even job growth at just over 5,000 a month — [ABC11 (AP), Nov 6, 2026](https://abc11.com/story/us-employers-added-surprising-165000-jobs-october-unemployment-rate-dipped-41/20020018/)

- Debt service: total 11.11% of DPI in Q2 2026 (11.73% in Q4 2019; 15.85% in Q4 2007; series low 9.05%); consumer DSR 5.28% (5.81% in Q4 2019); mortgage DSR 5.83% (5.92% in Q4 2019) — [FRED TDSP](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/TDSP); [FRED CDSP](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CDSP); [FRED MDSP](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/MDSP)

- Early stress markers: small rise in card minimum-payment holders and slight increase in 401(k) hardship distributions in Q3 2026 — [BofA Institute, Nov 2026 (PDF)](https://institute.bankofamerica.com/content/dam/economic-insights/consumer-checkpoint-november-2026.pdf)
- Sentiment: University of Michigan index 48.1 in September 2026, versus 55.1 in September 2025 and a 2026 low of 44.8 in May — [FRED UMCSENT](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/UMCSENT)
- Research basis for discounting the level: correct first prints for average upward bias before reacting — [Croushore et al.](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~dcrousho/docs/PSR_2022Apr21.pdf)

### Inferences
- A defensible warning framework from the evidence: a low saving rate becomes a warning when (1) it persists after annual revisions, (2) real DPI growth is at or below zero while real spending still grows, for two or more quarters, (3) unemployment starts rising, (4) lower-income liquid balances fall toward or below 2019 real levels, and (5) debt service or delinquency flows rise. As of November 2026, (2) is close to met (real DPI +1.0% y/y, -0.3% m/m in September, 0.0% in August), (5) is partly met on delinquency but not on debt service, and (1), (3), (4) are not met.
- The most informative near-term variables are the unemployment rate (4.1%, stable) and equity and house prices, because the top of the distribution is funding spending from wealth and the bottom from wages.
- With inflation at 3.6% and the Fed tightening, the benign 2022-style exit (inflation falls, real incomes recover) depends on energy prices retreating; the less benign path is a forced slowdown in spending as Oxford Economics expects.

## 6. Macro backdrop for households in 2026

### Takeaway
Households face a stagflation-leaning mix: inflation of 3.6% to 3.7% driven by an oil shock, falling real hourly wages, a low-hire but low-layoff labor market with unemployment at 4.1%, strong headline GDP, and a Federal Reserve that has turned to rate increases with more priced in.
