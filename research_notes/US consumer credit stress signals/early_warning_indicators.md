## 1. Labor market: latest readings, trend, and the empirical link between unemployment and card charge-offs

### Takeaway
None of the classic labor-market triggers is flashing in autumn 2026: unemployment is 4.1% (October), the Sahm indicator is at roughly zero, initial claims are about 200,000, layoffs and Challenger cuts are at multi-year lows, and quits and openings rose in September. The weak spots are the ones that hit household cash flow rather than employment: real hourly earnings were falling year over year by September (-0.7%), involuntary part-time work jumped by 339,000 in October, and households' own expectations of higher unemployment are the highest since April 2020.

In the 2024 episode it triggered at 0.53 (July 2024) and peaked at 0.57 (August 2024) with no recession. — [thetrading.tools, Aug 17, 2026](https://www.thetrading.tools/chart-of-the-day/2026-08-17-sahm-rule-unwound)
- A tracker page shows the latest reading as 0.00 (page stamped October 2026; likely September data). — [macroradar.io](https://www.macroradar.io/sahm-rule)
- The Sahm rule triggered with the July 2024 report (unemployment 4.3%), switched off in October 2024, and is described by Northern Trust's Ryan Boyle as the rule's first false alarm. — [Fortune, Dec 23, 2024](https://fortune.com/2024/12/23/economists-wrong-on-recession-northern-trust-ryan-boyle-3-culprits/)
- Historically the rule "triggered at or shortly after the onset of each downturn" — it is a coincident confirmation, not a lead. — [macroradar.io](https://www.macroradar.io/sahm-rule)

Unemployment and card charge-offs
- Mercator Advisory Group found a "close alignment of credit losses to the unemployment rate": in 2006 unemployment was 4.7% and card charge-offs 3.5%; at the recession peak unemployment reached 9.9% and charge-offs 10.4%. Issuers "typically expect" losses of 3.5% to 4.0%. Recovery is asymmetric: charge-offs fell quickly after the purge of weak accounts while unemployment took nearly a decade to normalize. — [PaymentsJournal, Jul 7, 2020](https://www.paymentsjournal.com/unemployment-and-credit-losses-enough-to-force-change-in-credit-policy-through-2022/amp/)
- A loss-forecasting study on top-100-bank card charge-offs (Q1 1985–Q2 2019) finds unemployment has a 0.52 contemporaneous correlation with the loss rate and explains about 63% of variance alone, but initial unemployment claims rank highest in several models; the selected leading set is building permits, initial claims, M1, PMI, manufacturing weekly hours and unemployment. It also notes that from Q2 2018 to Q2 2019 "the unemployment rate was going down while the charge-off rate was increasing." — [arXiv 2006.07911](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.07911)
- Liu and Liang (Managerial Finance, 2023): "the credit card charge-off rate is primarily driven by permanent UR while temporary UR has little predictive power"; permanent-layoff unemployment is the stronger indicator in recessions. — [University of Illinois Experts page](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/permanent-layoff-and-consumer-credit-card-loss-forecasting/)
- San Francisco Fed (1996): card charge-offs move inversely with payroll employment growth and turning points in the two series were always within three quarters of each other; banks' willingness to make consumer loans in the loan officer survey turned down shortly before charge-offs turned up in 1994–95. — [FRBSF Economic Letter](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/economic-letter/1996/07/what-is-behind-problem-credit-card-loans/)
- Actual bank data (collected CSV): card charge-off rate, all commercial banks, 3.82% in Q2 2026 (4.18% in Q3 2025; cycle peak 4.69% in Q3 2024; 3.78% in Q4 2019; 10.54% in Q4 2009). — [FRED CORCCACBS](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CORCCACBS)

### Inferences
- The simple rule of thumb that the card charge-off rate runs close to the unemployment rate roughly holds today (4.1% unemployment in October 2026 versus 3.82% charge-offs in Q2 2026). The Mercator endpoints imply about 1.3 points of charge-off per point of unemployment in 2006–2010 (derived: 6.9-point rise in charge-offs over a 5.2-point rise in unemployment). On that slope, the KPMG bear case (unemployment 5.4% by end-2027, about 6% in 2028) would imply card charge-offs moving from roughly 3.8% to somewhere around 5.5%–6.5% (my arithmetic, not a sourced forecast).
- The 2022–2024 rise in charge-offs (1.63% to 4.69%) happened with no rise in unemployment, and 2018–19 showed the same decoupling. The unemployment rule explains recession losses but not underwriting/seasoning-driven losses; a watch list needs both labor and credit-behavior indicators.
- Order in which labor data would likely turn, based on the lead times found: temp help and hours first (11–16 months ahead in 2001 and 2007), then quits/openings and claims, then the Sahm rule (coincident). Today temp help is flat-to-up, quits rose, claims are low. The only labor series moving the wrong way are involuntary part-time work, real wages, and household unemployment expectations.
- Low layoffs plus low permanent hiring ("low-hire, low-fire") means the claims series will not warn early about people who cannot find work; continuing claims, long-term unemployment share (27.6%) and job-finding expectations are the better checks.

## 2. Credit behavior indicators that lead losses: latest readings versus pre-2008 and 2019

### Takeaway
Card credit behavior is improving, not deteriorating: early-stage card delinquency transitions (8.39% annualized in Q3 2026) are the lowest since Q3 2023, large-bank 30+ day delinquency fell to 2.99% in Q2 2026 (about its 2019 level), full-payers match the pandemic-era peak at 37% of accounts, and utilization is at a three-year low. The stress is migrating to autos and mortgages (record 90+ day auto balances, highest mortgage early-delinquency transition since 2015) and to household expectations: the expected probability of missing a debt payment jumped to 13.8% in October 2026.

### Inferences
- On the card indicators that led losses in past cycles (early delinquency flows, minimum-payment share, utilization, revolving balance growth), the latest readings are flat to better and broadly back at 2019 levels. They do not corroborate a near-term rise in card losses.
- Two things cut against that comfort. First, the data lag: Y-14M and bank data end in Q2 2026 and Q3 flows cover July–September, while gasoline spiked and real incomes turned negative in August–October. Second, the forward-looking survey measure (missed-payment probability, +1.6 points in one month) and the Q3 jump in overall delinquent share (+0.23 points to 4.96%) are the first numbers to turn.
- Borrower-count measures (TransUnion 2.50% versus 2.37%) are worsening while balance-weighted measures improve: growth in high-score balances is diluting the ratio. Watch account-based rates and lower-income/young cohorts, not just balance-weighted aggregates.
- The rise in sub-660 originations since mid-2025 (16.1% to 18.5–19.2%) is the same mechanism the Fed blames for 2022–24 losses; such vintages typically show up in losses several quarters later. This is the most credible slow-building card signal.
- The rejection rate rising back to 18.3% while the application rate falls and voluntary closures hit a record suggests households are retrenching and lenders are quietly tightening at the margin after an easing phase in H1 2026.

## 3. Spending and income signals

### Takeaway
Nominal spending looks strong but it is price-driven: in September 2026 real disposable income fell 0.3%, real spending was flat, the saving rate dropped to 3.8% (from 5.1% a year earlier), and gasoline accounted for almost all of the 0.8% retail sales gain. Spending growing faster than income, financed by lower saving, is the pattern to worry about; the offset is record household net worth concentrated at the top.

### Inferences
- The gap between nominal spending growth (+6.3% year over year) and nominal disposable income growth (+4.6%) is about 1.6 points and is being closed by a falling saving rate (5.1% to 3.8% in a year). ## 5. What the indicators looked like before credit turned in 2006–07, what is flashing today, and the 2022–23 false alarms

### Takeaway
Before 2007–09, the earliest moves came from temp help (16 months ahead), bankruptcy filings and card charge-offs drifting up through 2007 from a 2006 trough, and a record debt service burden — while the headline card delinquency rate was actually falling until mid-2007. In 2022–23 the yield curve, two negative GDP quarters, falling temp help and later the Sahm rule all gave false recession signals, yet card losses still nearly tripled from their trough without any rise in unemployment. Today the labor and card-behavior indicators are quiet; what is flashing is the real-income squeeze, a falling saving rate, auto and mortgage delinquency flows, expected missed payments, and looser recent card underwriting.

- Answer to "what would we see first": based on 2006–07 and the lead times found, the order would be (1) real income and saving-rate deterioration plus rising survey expectations of missed payments — already happening; (2) rising early-delinquency flows in autos and then cards, and rising account-based (not balance-weighted) delinquency — autos already, cards not yet; (3) lenders tightening terms, then headline standards — terms already; (4) temp help, hours and quits falling and claims rising — not yet; (5) unemployment and the Sahm rule — last, and by then charge-offs are already rising.
- Answer to "when should we become concerned": the 2022–23 lesson is that price and rate squeezes alone raise losses gradually (card charge-offs went from 1.6% to 4.7% with no recession) but do not produce a 2008-style spike without job losses. Concern should step up when a labor signal joins the cash-flow signals: for example claims and continuing claims trending up for several weeks alongside falling quits and temp help, or card early-delinquency transitions rising for two quarters while the saving rate is below 3.5%. Today cash-flow signals are flashing and labor signals are not.
- Why false alarms happened in 2022–23 and how that maps to today: then, households had excess savings, locked-in low mortgage rates, strong nominal wage gains and a labor-supply-driven rise in unemployment, so recession signals from the yield curve, GDP and temp help did not translate into layoffs. Several of those cushions are thinner now — saving rate 3.8% versus 4.3% in January 2022, real wages falling, the Fed hiking again, and about 20% of families behind on a payment in 2025 — but debt service ratios (11.1%) remain far below 2007 (15.85%) and wealth is at a record. (The excess-savings and locked-in-mortgage explanation is my synthesis; the Fortune source covers only the indicator failures.)
- A notable difference from 2006–07: then headline card delinquency was falling while charge-offs and bankruptcies rose; today both are falling. A similar divergence to watch for is account-based delinquency rising while balance-weighted rates fall — TransUnion already shows it.
