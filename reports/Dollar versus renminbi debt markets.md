# Cheap yuan debt masks weak demand

The starting hypothesis, that low renminbi yields hand China a cheap-debt advantage for building infrastructure, technology and AI, is right about the price of money and largely wrong about what that price means. As of 17 November 2026 China's 10-year government yield is about 1.62% against 5.12% for the US, a gap of roughly 3.5 percentage points, and top Chinese technology firms borrow in renminbi at 2% to 3% while Meta has just paid 5.85% to 7.50% in dollars. But China's credit is not accelerating: outstanding loan growth hit a record low of 4.7% in October 2026, fixed-asset investment is down 8.1% year to date, and the debt that is growing is overwhelmingly government debt. The evidence points to low yields as a symptom of weak private demand and savings held at home by capital controls, with the cheapest money reaching the state and state-linked borrowers first, and with China's AI spending running at roughly one-sixth of the US level and constrained more by chips and returns than by interest rates. In size the two markets are closer than most people assume inside their borders (China's whole onshore bond market, about $31 trillion, is almost exactly the size of the US Treasury market alone) and nowhere near each other outside them: foreigners hold about 29% of Treasuries and 1.8% of Chinese bonds. The claim that China is the only major economy with falling yields holds over one and five years across the eleven economies checked, with Switzerland the exception over three years. The largest real change in 2026 is not in who holds renminbi but in who borrows it: foreign and offshore issuance is at records while foreign holdings of Chinese bonds have shrunk.

## China's whole bond market equals the US Treasury market alone, but foreigners own 29% of one and 1.8% of the other

Start with the stock of debt. **US marketable Treasuries outstanding were $31.8 trillion in September 2026**, up 7.1% in a year and roughly double the $16.7 trillion at end-2019 ([SIFMA Treasury statistics](https://www.sifma.org/research/statistics/us-treasury-securities-statistics); [SIFMA Fixed Income Outstanding 2Q26](https://www.sifma.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/SIFMA-Research-Quarterly-Fixed-Income-O-2Q26.pdf)). Adding corporates ($12.1 trillion), municipals, agency debt and commercial paper, SIFMA's US total excluding mortgage- and asset-backed securities was $51.4 trillion at mid-2026 ([SIFMA Fixed Income Outstanding 2Q26](https://www.sifma.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/SIFMA-Research-Quarterly-Fixed-Income-O-2Q26.pdf)). **China's onshore bond market stood at CNY 210.8 trillion at end-September 2026** ([Xinhua Finance](https://www.cnfin.com/zs-lb/detail/20261026/4486510_1.html)), which converts to about $31.4 trillion at the official end-September rate of 6.7053 yuan per dollar ([People's Daily on the PBoC Q3 report](https://en.people.cn/n3/2026/1112/c90000-20518367.html)); the conversion is this report's arithmetic. It was CNY 99.1 trillion at end-2019 ([China Daily](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202001/19/WS5e240b94a310128217272077.html)). Both markets have therefore roughly doubled in seven years. The first surprise in the data is that the two countries' domestic debt markets are of the same order of magnitude.

The resemblance ends at the border. The table sets the two side by side; figures are the latest found in the research notes, and dollar conversions of renminbi amounts are either the source's own or flagged.

The 29% foreign share of Treasuries is arithmetic on mismatched months and is approximate. The SWIFT dollar share is 52.09% on the tracker's second measure, and the notes could not confirm which measure is which.

Several of these numbers need care. Estimates of the dim sum (offshore renminbi) bond stock range from CNY 1.3 trillion to CNY 2.6 trillion depending on the compiler and what is counted, and no Bank for International Settlements series for renminbi credit abroad was found, so the offshore renminbi total is an order-of-magnitude figure. Stacking Chinese banks' overseas renminbi loans and the dim sum and panda bond stock against the $14.7 trillion of offshore dollar credit suggests renminbi credit to non-residents is around 5% of the dollar equivalent, a rough estimate that mixes sources.

Who holds the debt is shifting on the dollar side. Foreign holdings of Treasuries were flat over the twelve months to August 2026 (up 0.9%) while the market grew about 7%, so foreigners absorbed almost none of the year's net supply. Within that total, **foreign official holders cut $164 billion** to $3,755.6 billion, while private holders booked through the UK and other financial centres added more ([Treasury TIC Table 5](https://ticdata.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/tic/Documents/slt_table5.html)). **China's reported holdings fell to $590.7 billion, 55% below the November 2013 peak** and the first reading under $600 billion since 2008, though custody through Belgium and other centres means the reported fall overstates the true decline by an unknown amount ([PrimeRates](https://primerates.com/china-cuts-treasury-holdings-590-7-billion-lowest-since-2008/)). Japan's holdings dropped from $1,239.3 billion in February to $1,068.3 billion in August, which coincides with a record ¥15.4 trillion ($96.5 billion) of yen-support intervention between 30 July and 26 August 2026; that is reserve use, not a judgment on US credit ([Bloomberg via BNN](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/business/economics/2026/08/28/japan-spent-record-965-billion-to-support-yen-over-past-month-ministry-data-shows/)). The foreign share of Treasuries has slid from almost 50% in 2014 to 32% in early 2025 ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/the-international-role-of-the-u-s-dollar-2025-edition-20250718.html)). Domestic buyers filled the gap: US money market funds added about $1.25 trillion of Treasuries between end-2023 and end-2025 ([Fed Z.1 table L.210](https://www.federalreserve.gov/RELEASES/z1/20260319/html/l210.htm)), and the Fed's own bill holdings rose $385 billion in the year to November 2026 ([Fed H.4.1](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/h41/20261112/)).

The money leaving Treasuries at the margin did not go to Chinese bonds. Foreign investors were net sellers of onshore renminbi bonds for eleven straight months to March 2026, cutting holdings by about CNY 1.25 trillion or 28% ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-04-16/foreign-investors-keep-selling-chinese-bonds-despite-resilience)), with the first monthly rise only in May 2026 ([Reuters via StratNews Global](https://stratnewsglobal.com/world-news/china-sees-first-foreign-bond-inflows-since-april-2025/)). The visible beneficiaries of reserve diversification are gold and the euro: the European Central Bank reported gold at 27% of global official reserves at end-2025 against 22% for US Treasuries ([Northern Miner citing the ECB](https://www.northernminer.com/news/gold-overtakes-us-treasuries-in-global-reserve-shift-ecb/1003891748/)), and euro-denominated international debt securities grew 13% in the year to June 2026 against 6.7% for dollar ones ([BIS commentary, October 2026](https://www.bis.org/publications/202610-commentary-ibs-gli)). The renminbi's reserve share, about 2%, is below its 2.8% peak in 2022 ([RBA Bulletin](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2026/aug/developments-in-rmb-internationalisation.html)).

Two details complicate the standard story that Chinese yields are low because of deflation. First, **Chinese producer prices are no longer falling: they rose 5.7% in the year to October 2026**, a more-than-four-year high driven by energy and metals, while consumer prices rose 0.7% and core consumer prices 0.8% ([National Bureau of Statistics](https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/PressRelease/202611/t20261111_1965806.html)). Yields fell to a record anyway, alongside retail sales growth of 0.4% and falling home prices ([Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/china/government-bond-yield/news/599819)). That pattern fits imported cost inflation sitting on top of weak domestic demand. Second, the renminbi has appreciated despite the record rate gap, from 7.30 per dollar at end-2024 to 6.99 at end-2025 and about 6.67 now ([Alpha Vantage FX data](https://www.alphavantage.co/documentation/)). A low-yielding currency that rises is unusual, and it matters for everything that follows.

Both governments are borrowing heavily. The difference lies in what is happening around them.

In the US, the Treasury financed a $1.99 trillion deficit with a shift towards bills, which supplied about 35% of net borrowing in FY2026 against about 17% the year before. Demand has not failed, but it is changing hands and getting more expensive. The 11 November 10-year auction cleared at 5.02% with a 5% coupon, the highest on a 10-year since 2006, and indirect bidders took 61.2% against 80.3% in October, the lowest since October 2023 ([CapWolf](https://capwolf.com/ugly-10y-treasury-auction-shows-weak-foreign-demand/)). Indirect bidding is an imperfect proxy for foreign demand, and bid-to-cover stayed above 2.4, so this is a change in who bought, not a failed sale. Corporate America is borrowing at a record pace at the same time: **US investment-grade issuance reached $1.76 trillion by late October, passing the 2020 full-year record**, with more than $300 billion of AI-related funding raised in global high-grade markets in 2026, nearly three times the 2025 total ([The Star/Bloomberg](https://www.thestar.com.my/business/business-news/2026/10/31/us-debts-raise-the-roof)). The US picture is heavy supply from both government and companies meeting price-sensitive buyers.

In China the government is the borrower of last resort. Converting 2025's CNY 13.8 trillion of net government bond financing at the roughly 7.04 yuan per dollar prevailing in December 2025 gives about $2 trillion, which is close to the US Treasury's net borrowing in a much smaller economy; that conversion is this report's. The International Monetary Fund's broad "augmented" measure, which includes local government financing vehicles, shows a deficit of about 14% of GDP and debt rising from 126.6% of GDP in 2025 to 135.3% in 2026 and 153.7% by 2030 ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/02/18/pr-26053-china-imf-executive-board-concludes-2025-article-iv-consultation)). On that measure China's debt ratio is rising several times faster than America's. Meanwhile private credit is contracting. In October 2026 new yuan loans fell by CNY 100 billion, both household and corporate loans were net repaid, and January to October new lending was CNY 11.42 trillion against CNY 14.97 trillion a year earlier; total social financing growth of 7.2% is being carried by government bonds ([The Standard/Reuters](https://www.thestandard.com.hk/finance/article/348457/Chinas-October-new-bank-loans-shrink-for-third-time-this-year-as-credit-demand-weakens)). Local government financing vehicles, the traditional funders of construction, had negative net bond financing onshore and offshore in the first half of 2026 under regulatory caps ([S&P Global China Ratings](https://www.spgchinaratings.cn/upload/20260818_LGFV%20Mid-Year%20Review-.pdf)).

The one place renminbi issuance is booming is across the border. Panda bond issuance in the first nine months of 2026 was already 134% of the 2025 total; in Hong Kong, renminbi bonds were 37% of listed bond issuance by value in the first nine months of 2026, ahead of the dollar's 33% ([China Daily HK](https://www.chinadailyhk.com/article/642680)); and Chinese banks' overseas lending in renminbi overtook their foreign-currency lending in early 2026 ([RBA Bulletin](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2026/aug/developments-in-rmb-internationalisation.html)). The borrowers named are European banks and industrials (Deutsche Bank, Barclays, BNP Paribas, Volkswagen, Henkel), sovereigns with trade or debt links to China (Kazakhstan, Pakistan, Indonesia, Slovenia, Portugal), and Chinese technology and state firms refinancing dollar debt ([SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/economy/china-economy/article/3356319/china-sees-record-panda-bond-issuance-2026-foreign-borrowers-pile); [The Standard/Reuters](https://www.thestandard.com.hk/finance/article/340116/From-kangaroos-to-dim-sum-foreign-borrowers-rush-into-Asia-Pacific-bond-markets)). Some of the growth is conversion of existing dollar debt, as with Kenya's switch of three railway loans, which changes the currency without adding credit ([The EastAfrican](https://theeastafrican.co.ke/tea/business-tech/five-countries-eye-kenya-style-switch-from-dollar-to-yuan-loans-5508096)). The renminbi is being adopted as a currency to borrow, not to hold.

## Testing the hypothesis: right on coupons, partly right on real cost, wrong on cause

The trail of reasoning runs in five steps, and the hypothesis survives the first and weakens at each one after.

| Claim tested | Verdict | Evidence in brief |
|---|---|---|
| Chinese borrowers pay much lower nominal rates | Right | Tencent and Baidu about 2.6% on long yuan bonds; Meta 5.85% to 7.50% in dollars |
| The real, inflation-adjusted cost is lower | Partly right | Similar on consumer prices (about 2% real each); far lower on producer prices, but that inflation is upstream energy |
| Cheap bonds are how China's AI build-out is funded | Partly right | Loans and equity dominate; 87% of Chinese hyperscaler bonds outstanding are legacy dollar bonds |
| Cheap debt reaches the innovators | Mostly wrong | 87% of 2025 "sci-tech" bonds by value came from state-owned issuers; private firms under 8% |
| Low yields are producing a debt-funded investment boom | Wrong | Loan growth at a record low, fixed-asset investment down 8.1%, AI capex about one-sixth of the US level |

**Step one: the coupon gap is real and large.** Tencent's September 2025 offshore renminbi deal priced at 2.1% for five years, 2.5% for ten and 3.1% for thirty ([SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/business/article/3325808/tencent-raise-us127-billion-first-dim-sum-bond-offering-it-joins-ai-race)); Baidu borrowed at 1.9% the same month ([KrASIA](https://kr-asia.com/baidu-raises-offshore-debt-at-record-low-rates-to-bankroll-its-ai-ambitions)). Rhodium Group found long-term yuan coupons for the two of about 2.6% in the first half of 2026 against about 4.4% average effective cost on US hyperscalers' long-term bonds, and a 2.8% rate on Tencent's long-term yuan loans against 5.1% on its dollar loans ([Rhodium Group](https://rhg.com/research/examining-chinas-ai-financing/)). Since then US costs have risen: Meta's November 2026 $15 billion deal carried 5.85% for 2031, 6.55% for 2036 and 7.50% for 2056 ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/meta-borrows-eur9bn-in-euros-at-up-to-6-4-until-2065/)), and CoreWeave paid 9.75% on unsecured notes in April ([Business Wire](https://stocks.observer-reporter.com/observerreporter/article/bizwire-2026-4-9-coreweave-announces-upsize-and-pricing-of-1750-million-of-senior-notes)). The like-for-like gap for top-tier names is now roughly 3.5 to 4 points, though that comparison mixes dates and tenors. State channels are cheaper still: the central bank's technology relending quota was raised to CNY 1.4 trillion and its one-year pledged supplementary lending rate cut to 1.5% in September 2026, and policy banks deployed CNY 800 billion of project capital instruments by late October ([CGTN](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-09-29/PBOC-announces-adjustments-to-several-monetary-policy-tools-1QPWGj2poxG/p.html); [CGTN](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-10-29/China-issues-800-bln-yuan-via-financial-instruments-1RDjKawrULm/p.html)). The Chinese state itself funds at 1.6% for ten years. On price alone, the hypothesis is right.

**Step two: adjust for inflation and the advantage depends on the yardstick.** Deflated by consumer prices, a 2.6% Chinese tech coupon costs about 1.9% in real terms and Meta's 5.85% five-year about 2.2%, so much of the nominal gap is an inflation differential. Deflated by producer prices the Chinese figure is about minus 3.1%, a reversal from 2025 when falling factory prices made Chinese real rates high. But the producer-price rise is concentrated in mining, energy and raw materials, while factory-gate prices of consumer goods are still down 0.1% ([National Bureau of Statistics](https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/PressRelease/202611/t20261111_1965806.html)), so firms selling cloud services or consumer products do not get that relief. Any real-rate claim about China needs a date and a deflator attached.

**Step three: bonds are a minor channel for China's AI spending.** Rhodium's survey of thirteen Chinese AI-related firms shows net loan inflows of CNY 639 billion in 2025 against net bond inflows of CNY 341 billion, with equity the leading channel in 2026: Alibaba announced an HK$80 billion share placement in August, and frontier-lab financing jumped from CNY 9 billion in 2025 to CNY 179 billion in the first eight months of 2026 ([Rhodium Group](https://rhg.com/research/examining-chinas-ai-financing/); [technode citing Moody's](https://technode.global/2026/08/28/chinese-hyperscalers-ramp-up-ai-spending-but-trail-us-rivals-moodys/)). ByteDance's reported $29.6 billion syndicated loan in September 2026 was offshore. The flagship onshore programme, the 2025 "technology board", produced about CNY 2.3 trillion of sci-tech bonds, but state-owned issuers supplied 87% by value, private firms under 8%, and a Caixin summary that could not be verified against the full article puts over half the proceeds into repaying or swapping debt ([Caixin](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-04-03/in-depth-chinas-sci-tech-bond-boom-conceals-sidelined-private-sector-102430643.html)). The cheapest money reaches state entities and designated projects first; state telecom operators won more than half of large computing tenders in January to July 2026 ([technode](https://technode.com/2026/11/11/china-accelerates-data-center-expansion-as-the-west-faces-mounting-opposition/)).

**Step four: scale.** Moody's puts leading Chinese firms' AI capital spending at about $140 billion in 2026 against more than $785 billion for six US hyperscalers ([technode citing Moody's](https://technode.global/2026/08/28/chinese-hyperscalers-ramp-up-ai-spending-but-trail-us-rivals-moodys/)). US hyperscalers' bond issuance alone, $132 billion to $194 billion in the first seven months of 2026 depending on the count, is of the same order as China's entire AI capex ([Chief Investment Officer](https://www.ai-cio.com/?p=106712); [Reuters via WDEZ](https://wdez.com/2026/07/29/hyperscaler-debt-binge-pushes-yields-up-as-investor-demand-cools/)). The more expensive market is funding the far larger build-out. What the sources identify as binding in China is chip access and returns: Alibaba's cloud margin is about 10% against 39% for Amazon's, and only 13.6% of intelligent-computing projects were operating in the first nine months of 2026 ([Rhodium Group](https://rhg.com/research/examining-chinas-ai-financing/); [technode](https://technode.com/2026/11/11/china-accelerates-data-center-expansion-as-the-west-faces-mounting-opposition/)). The US market shows strain of its own: 78 of 91 hyperscaler bonds issued in 2026 yielded more by late July than at issue, and order-book cover fell from nearly five times in February to under two in July ([Reuters via WDEZ](https://wdez.com/2026/07/29/hyperscaler-debt-binge-pushes-yields-up-as-investor-demand-cools/)).

**Step five: construction.** The broad build-out the hypothesis imagines is not visible in the aggregates. Fixed-asset investment is down 8.1% year to date, new-home prices are falling, land-sale revenue has roughly halved from its 2021 peak, and the vehicles that financed local construction are repaying debt ([Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/china/government-bond-yield/news/599819); [Rhodium Group, February 2026](https://rhg.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/RHG_China_FinancialFiscalDecay_19Feb2026.pdf)). What exists is targeted, state-directed investment (the National Development and Reform Commission estimates "six networks" investment above CNY 7 trillion in 2026) inside a shrinking total.

## What the evidence points to instead: trapped savings, absent borrowers, and a bill paid by banks and savers

If low yields are not driving a boom, what are they? The sources converge on a demand deficit. The IMF describes private domestic demand as "lackluster" and deflationary pressure as "persistent", with national saving at 42.1% of GDP ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/02/18/pr-26053-china-imf-executive-board-concludes-2025-article-iv-consultation)). A Reuters column quotes Barclays to the effect that on fiscal metrics alone China's yields should be higher than the West's, and describes the bid for bonds as "trapped money with nowhere else to go" behind capital controls after a property slump ([Reuters via Metrobank](https://wealthinsights.metrobank.com.ph/news/rpt-roi-china-escapes-global-bond-rout-but-for-the-wrong-reasons-mcgeever)). Rhodium finds 58% of loans in December 2025 priced at or below the 3% loan prime rate, with 18 of the 20 largest additions to corporate debt in 2025 going to state borrowers, and concludes that lower rates have not raised lending volumes ([Rhodium Group](https://rhg.com/research/the-banks-behind-the-china-shock/)). The central bank's own behaviour supports the reading: in 2024 and 2025 it paused bond purchases and warned investors to stop yields falling too far, which is not how an authority treats an advantage ([MNI](https://www.mnimarkets.com/mni-pboc-to-increase-cgb-purchases-add-longer-tenors-1763097613639)).

The steelman for the original hypothesis deserves stating. A state that can direct a captive banking system and a closed savings pool to fund government and favoured sectors at 1.5% to 3% has a real capability, and China runs an augmented deficit of 14% of GDP without a funding problem because its sovereign rate sits well below nominal growth. The US, by contrast, pays about 5% at the margin, already spends about a fifth of federal receipts on interest, and the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget warns that its average interest rate will exceed growth later this decade ([CRFB summary of CBO](https://www.crfb.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/CRFB_CBO_February_2026_Budget_And_Economic_Outlook_4.pdf)). So low yields are an advantage to the Chinese government as a debtor. They are a much weaker advantage to the private economy, because the subsidy is paid by someone inside China: bank net interest margins hit a record low of 1.4% in March 2026 ([Caixin](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-05-18/chinese-banks-net-interest-margin-hits-record-low-102444983.html)), the state injected about CNY 290 billion into its banks in 2026 ([Rhodium Group](https://rhg.com/research/the-banks-behind-the-china-shock/)), regulators relaxed insurers' duration-matching rules ([Caixin](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-08-24/china-throws-insurers-lifeline-as-falling-rates-squeeze-returns-102477460.html)), and depositors earn little, which weighs on the consumption that is already missing. Analysts who use the Japan parallel make the same point: decades of very low yields there accompanied private deleveraging, not an investment boom ([Bruegel](https://www.bruegel.org/newsletter/what-can-china-learn-japans-lost-decade)). The notes contain no Japanese yield series, so the parallel rests on those analysts' accounts.

The cross-border evidence fits this reading. A currency that is cheap to borrow and unrewarding to hold produces exactly the 2026 pattern: record foreign issuance, shrinking foreign holdings, and a reserve share below its 2022 peak. The currency's rise then limits even the borrowing advantage. The renminbi gained about 4.4% against the dollar in 2025 and about 4.6% so far in 2026, which offsets most of a 2 to 3 point coupon saving for an unhedged dollar-based borrower, and Portugal's April 2026 panda bond, swapped into euros, delivered only a small saving ([Caixin](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-04-21/in-depth-dim-sum-bonds-shift-toward-main-course-from-niche-appetizer-102436383.html); [The Standard/Reuters](https://www.thestandard.com.hk/finance/article/340116/From-kangaroos-to-dim-sum-foreign-borrowers-rush-into-Asia-Pacific-bond-markets)). The only US name found among renminbi issuers is Morgan Stanley; no US technology company was found borrowing in renminbi. The constraints on a larger international role are the ones official sources list: capital controls, a managed exchange rate, shallow offshore markets (an offshore liquidity pool of about CNY 1 trillion) and a shortage of safe renminbi assets that foreigners can freely access ([RBA Bulletin](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2026/aug/developments-in-rmb-internationalisation.html)).

## What to watch next

The near-term calendar is dense. The Federal Reserve meets on 8 to 9 December with a rise widely priced, and the 20 November loan prime rate fixing will show whether Chinese banks pass the new 1.25% overnight benchmark through to borrowers after seventeen months without change. Treasury data on foreign holdings for September 2026 had not been located as of 17 November; it will show whether official selling continued and whether China's reported holdings fell further below $600 billion. The Treasury expects $618 billion of net marketable borrowing in October to December and $800 billion in January to March 2027 ([Treasury presentation to TBAC](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/221/TreasuryPresentationToTBACQ42026.pdf)), so the indirect-bidder share at coming 10-year and 30-year auctions is the cleanest running test of demand at 5%.

In China, four indicators would signal that the low-yield regime is changing. The first is whether 5.7% producer inflation passes into consumer prices, which would be the one route by which the central bank is pushed to tighten. The second is loan growth: a turn up from 4.7% would mean private borrowers are returning, which is what would have to happen for cheap money to become a true advantage. The third is the renminbi itself, since continued appreciation erodes the appeal of renminbi borrowing for foreigners while a reversal would revive it. The fourth is capital-account policy: any loosening would let savings chase 5% dollar yields and lift Chinese rates, and the next review of the IMF's reserve-currency basket is due by July 2027 ([Atlantic Council](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/blogs/econographics/the-imfs-boost-to-chinas-currency-is-costing-the-world-ten-years-later/)). On the technology side, the test is whether Chinese firms' free cash flow, which swung from CNY 170 billion in 2025 to minus CNY 16 billion in the first half of 2026 for Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu, pushes them towards the bond market in size ([Rhodium Group](https://rhg.com/research/examining-chinas-ai-financing/)). Model-based forecasts on hand imply only marginal narrowing of the gap over twelve months, to roughly 4.9% for the US and 1.6% for China, and no source read forecasts a reversal ([Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/united-states/government-bond-yield)).

Several gaps limit confidence. The breakdown of China's bond stock by type and by domestic holder was not found, nor were Chinese corporate bond yields, gross Chinese issuance, hedging costs between the two currencies, or a term-premium reading after May 2026. Some figures rest on paywalled summaries or data aggregators and are flagged where used.

## Conclusion

The most useful reframing is that the two yield levels are answers to different questions. A 5% Treasury yield reflects an economy where the government, companies and an AI investment cycle are all competing for capital during an inflation shock; a 1.6% Chinese yield reflects an economy where the government is close to the only willing borrower. Each number carries its own strain. The US pays for abundant demand for capital with a rising interest bill and a buyer base that is becoming more domestic and more price-sensitive. China pays for cheap government funding with squeezed banks, insurers and savers, and with debt on the broad measure rising faster than America's. Neither rate, taken alone, measures strength.

For the original question, the practical lesson is to separate three things the hypothesis ran together: the Chinese state's cost of debt, which is a real advantage; Chinese private firms' cost of capital, which is lower in coupon terms but is not the binding limit on what they build; and the renminbi's role abroad, which is growing fastest as a liability currency for foreign borrowers. The last of these is the new development of 2026 and the one most sensitive to change, because it depends on a rate gap and a currency trend that both sit near extremes.
