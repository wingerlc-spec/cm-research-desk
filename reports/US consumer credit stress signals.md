# Card losses fall while household cushions thin

As of mid-November 2026, issuer-level data do not show a deteriorating American card borrower: all eight large issuers reviewed reported lower Q3 2026 card losses than a year earlier (seven with a published rate, Wells Fargo in words only), and the October monthly filings from Synchrony, Capital One and Bread remain below October 2025. The two signals that prompted this work turned out to say less than they appear to. The smaller-bank delinquency rate of 6.49% describes roughly 3% of bank card balances held largely by subprime specialists, and the gap to large banks opened in a single quarter of 2017 through a change in who is in the group, so the hypothesis that it signals broad distress is wrong, although it remains a useful magnifier of the nonprime segment. The saving-rate hypothesis is partly right: the fall from 5.1% to 3.8% is real and reflects households spending through an oil-driven price shock with real income growing only about 1%, but the series is revised heavily, has been a poor timing signal on its own, and is better read as a measure of shrinking slack than as a forecast. Pulled apart, the stress is narrow and deep rather than broad: autos, FHA mortgages, student loans, borrowers under 30 and nonprime borrowers are at or near post-2008 extremes, while prime cards and conventional mortgages are intact. Concern should step up when a labor-market signal joins the cash-flow signals that are already flashing, or when card early-delinquency flows rise for two consecutive quarters; neither has happened. The more likely source of higher lender losses in 2027 without a recession is the reopening of lower-score lending now under way, the same mechanism that took card charge-offs from 1.6% to 4.7% in 2022 to 2024 with no rise in unemployment. This report describes and analyzes; it is not a recommendation to buy or sell any security.

The largest improvements came from the highest-loss books: Bread down 99 basis points, Capital One down 65, Citi private label down 48 and Synchrony down 37. The level gap between prime and private-label lenders persists (Bread 6.40% and Synchrony 4.79% against JPMorgan 2.91% and American Express 1.8%), but it is narrowing rather than widening. The October monthly filings extend the pattern. **Synchrony reported October 30+ day delinquency of 4.4% against 4.5% a year earlier and an adjusted net charge-off rate of 4.6% against 5.3%** ([Synchrony monthly 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001601712/000160171226000045/creditstatsfinancialtables.htm)), and Bread reported a 6.69% loss rate against 7.50% ([Bread October 2026 credit stats](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001101215/000110121526000102/october2026creditstatsex99.htm)). Capital One's October domestic card charge-off rate of 4.02% and delinquency rate of 3.87% come from a secondary write-up because the filing itself could not be opened ([rallies.ai](https://rallies.ai/news/capital-one-reports-october-domestic-credit-card-net-charge-off-rate-of-402-22c85c27da7b7a48)). The small month-on-month rises in delinquency at all three are the normal autumn pattern.

Against 2019, Synchrony's Q3 2026 charge-off rate sits 56 basis points below its Q3 2019 rate of 5.35% and its 30+ day delinquency 13 points below 4.47% ([Synchrony Q3 2019 release](https://www.synchrony.com/contenthub/newsroom/synchrony-financial-reports-third-quarter-net-earnings-of-2019.html)); Capital One's domestic card loss rate is 14 points below its Q3 2019 rate of 4.12% ([Capital One Q3 2019 release](https://ir-capitalone.gcs-web.com/news-releases/news-release-details/capital-one-reports-third-quarter-2019-net-income-13-billion-or)). Both comparisons flatter the present, since Synchrony's 2019 book still held the Walmart portfolio and Capital One's 2026 book includes the lower-loss Discover portfolio. At industry level the Federal Reserve's charge-off rate for the 100 largest banks was 3.70% in Q2 2026, inside its 2019 range of 3.54% to 3.77% ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CORCCT100S)).

The harder question is how much of this reflects healthier borrowers. No source quantifies the split for losses, but the evidence that a large share is compositional is strong, and the clearest statement comes from Synchrony itself. Its payment rate rose about 60 basis points year on year to 16.9%, and its chief financial officer said **"over 90% of that or 56 basis points is really mix related"**; non-prime is "a couple of 100 basis points lower" as a share of the book ([Synchrony Q3 2026 call transcript](https://www.webull.com/news/15781703289185280)). Bread credits "further maturation of higher-quality new account acquisitions" ([Bread release](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001101215/000110121526000087/exh9913q26bfhepr_final.htm)). Industry data agree: the share of new card accounts going to scores below 660 fell from 25.2% in Q4 2021 to 16.4% in Q4 2025 ([KBRA](https://www.kbra.com/analytics/products/kfi/insights/credit-card-boom-slows-as-borrower-quality-remains-resilient)). The portfolios improved by selection at the top more than by recovery at the bottom.

That matters because the selection is now reversing. Synchrony's chief executive said "we started to open up a bit earlier in the year just given how good credit was looking" ([call transcript](https://www.webull.com/news/15781703289185280)); Capital One raised marketing 14% and plans more in 2027 ([Zacks call summary](https://www.zacks.com/stock/news/3011873/cof-q3-earnings-call-highlights-integration-and-spending-plans)); and the share of new large-bank card accounts with scores below 660 rose to 18.5% in Q2 2026 from 16.1% a year earlier, though with median lines of only about $600 ([Philadelphia Fed](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/surveys-and-data/2026-q2-large-bank)). Reserves no longer track losses either: Synchrony built $30 million against a $152 million release a year earlier and told investors not to expect the usual fourth-quarter decline in coverage, Bread built $21 million, and Citi built $208 million in US cards "primarily driven by changes in the macroeconomic outlook" ([Synchrony 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001601712/000160171226000043/syf-20260930.htm); [Citi transcript](https://www.citigroup.com/rcs/citigpa/storage/public/Earnings/Q32026/3q26-citigroup-earnings-transcript.pdf)). The only explicit 2027 comments say improvement slows at Bread and that Synchrony will see "some higher probably net charge offs" as its Walmart (OnePay) program seasons over about three vintages. Synchrony's 2026 guidance is for net charge-offs below 5.5%; no issuer gave numeric 2027 loss guidance in the sources reached.

The research notes contain nothing on Nu Holdings. Nu's borrowers are in Latin America, so none of the US readings here measure its credit quality directly, and this report draws no conclusion about it.

## The small-bank gap measures who the lenders are

The first hypothesis was that card delinquency at smaller banks, which is far above the large-bank rate and above its own 2009 level, shows consumer distress that the big issuers' numbers hide. The Federal Reserve's series looks alarming: in Q2 2026, banks outside the top 100 by assets reported card delinquency of **6.49% against 2.74% at the 100 largest**, and charge-offs of 7.80% against 3.70% ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DRCCLOBS)). Following the trail led to three findings that overturn the broad-distress reading.

First, the group is tiny. Dividing its annualized charge-off dollars by its charge-off rate implies roughly $33 billion of card balances, about 3% of the commercial-bank total; this is an estimate computed from Fed data, and independent estimates of "roughly 2.5%" and "a few percent" agree with it ([FRED charge-off dollars](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/NCOALLCCOB); [plaintext.media](https://www.plaintext.media/article/20260215_210053_NonTop100_Banks_CreditCard_ChargeOffs_Ha)). The all-bank delinquency rate of 2.85% sits almost on top of the large-bank rate, which shows the same thing.

Second, the gap is structural and dates from a composition change, not from borrower stress. In Q3 2017 the small-bank charge-off rate jumped from 4.68% to 7.89% in one quarter while the group's charge-off dollars fell from $239 million to $203 million. A rate can rise while losses fall only if the denominator shrinks sharply, which implies a large low-loss card book left the group that quarter. No source identifies the institution, and the St. Louis Fed reportedly said at the time that it did not know the cause ([Mother Jones](https://fullsite.motherjones.com/kevin-drum/2019/12/why-are-small-bank-credit-card-delinquencies-up)). The gap has been 3 to 5 points ever since.

Third, the comparison with 2009 is not a comparison of like with like. **At the crisis peak in Q2 2009, small banks had lower card delinquency than large banks, 4.60% against 6.87%.** The series has not measured the same kind of lender across time. Nor is the typical small issuer a high-cost subprime lender: the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau found small issuers charged borrowers with scores of 619 or below a median 20.62% against 28.49% at the largest 25 ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/credit-card-data-small-issuers-offer-lower-rates/)). The residual group behaves like a handful of subprime specialists. The popular explanation that small banks take more card risk because they lack other income also fails: a Bank Policy Institute study estimated the six largest issuers hold about 71% of subprime card balances, with subprime making up 32% of Capital One's balances and 28% of Synchrony's at end-2023 ([BPI](https://bpi.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Is-the-Subprime-Segment-of-the-Credit-Card-Market-Concentrated_.pdf)). That study came from a large-bank trade group, and the names of the lenders currently outside the top 100 were not verified.

The hypothesis is not wholly empty. The series does carry borrower information as a magnified view of the nonprime segment: it rose from 2021 to a peak of 7.86% in Q4 2023 and has fallen since, the same path the score-band data trace. And the score-band data settle the 2008 question directly. In the Fed's own figures for Q3 2025, **subprime card delinquency was 16.28% against a 2009 peak of 23.08%, near-prime 5.67% against 9.32%, and prime 1.28% against 2.36%** ([Federal Reserve FEDS Note data](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/a-note-on-recent-dynamics-of-consumer-delinquency-rates-accessible-20251124.htm)). Every tier is above its early-2020 level and none is near its crisis level. Tiered card data for 2026 were not found, so that reading is a year old.

What the exercise did surface is a subtler signal that an investor in consumer lenders should care about more than the small-bank headline. TransUnion's Q3 2026 report shows balance-weighted 90+ day card delinquency falling to 1.99% from 2.05% while the share of borrowers 90+ days late rose to 2.50% from 2.37%, with subprime originations up 16.5%; in unsecured personal loans, where fintechs originate about half the volume, borrower-level 60+ day delinquency rose to 4.12% from 3.52% while the balance-weighted rate fell ([TransUnion via Finviz](https://finviz.com/news/416965/credit-access-expands-as-lenders-pair-growth-with-more-strategic-risk-management)). More borrowers with small balances are falling behind while growth in good balances dilutes the ratio. Buy-now-pay-later debt cannot hide aggregate stress, at roughly $3 billion outstanding against $1.2 trillion of card debt, but it is concentrated in the same deep-subprime population ([Richmond Fed](https://richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_brief/2026/eb_26-05)).

## Households spent through an oil shock and let saving absorb it

The second hypothesis was that the falling saving rate shows households running out of room. The rate did fall, from 5.1% in September 2025 to **3.8% in September 2026**, the first reading below 4% since 2022, and almost all of the drop came after February 2026 ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PSAVERT); [BEA](https://bea.gov/news/2026/personal-income-and-outlays-september-2026)). The timing identifies the cause. The Iran war that began in late February 2026 disrupted Middle East oil exports ([Dallas Fed](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0417)); gasoline went from under $3 before the war to $4.48 in September ([AP via KSAT](https://www.ksat.com/business/2026/10/15/retail-sales-up-08-in-september-but-a-big-chunk-of-spending-went-to-paying-gasoline-bills/)), and PCE inflation reached 3.6%. Over the year to September, nominal spending grew 6.3% against 4.6% for disposable income; in real terms spending grew about 2.5% and income about 1.0% (computed from [FRED PCE](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PCE) and [DSPI](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DSPI)). Households held real consumption roughly on trend and let saving take the hit. Bank of America's card data show gasoline alone adding more than a point to spending growth ([BofA Institute](https://institute.bankofamerica.com/content/dam/economic-insights/consumer-checkpoint-november-2026.pdf)).

Two things allowed them to do it. Household net worth reached a record $195.9 trillion in Q2 2026, up 11.8% in a year ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/TNWBSHNO)), and layoffs stayed low. Two things often blamed did not drive it. The household debt service ratio was 11.11% of disposable income in Q2 2026, unchanged from a year earlier and well below 11.73% at end-2019 and 15.85% at end-2007 ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/TDSP)). Pandemic excess savings were exhausted years ago: the San Francisco Fed dates that to late 2021 for the bottom 80% by income and late 2022 for the top 20% ([SF Fed](https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/el2024-21.pdf)).

So the hypothesis is right that the cushion is thinner and wrong that the number itself is an alarm, for three reasons.

The level is unreliable in real time. The saving rate is a small residual between two very large numbers and is typically revised up as income data improve. April 2026 was first reported at 2.6%, described as the lowest since June 2022, and now stands at 4.4%; July was first reported at 3.0% and is now 4.5% ([Econostrum via Newswav](https://newswav.com/article/americans-are-running-out-of-cushion-savings-rates-fall-to-2022-lows-A2605_MbFujU); [Voice for Liberty](https://www.wichitaliberty.org/?p=41196)). A rule such as "below 3% is a warning" would have fired in spring 2026 and then been revised away. The same happened in the 2000s: 2005 was first published as a negative saving rate, yet the lowest reading in later vintages is 2.4%, and the academic study of these revisions concludes that "any alarm about a low rate of personal saving is likely misplaced" ([Croushore et al.](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~dcrousho/docs/PSR_2022Apr21.pdf)). The direction, by contrast, survived the September 2026 annual revision.

The history is one of poor timing. The rate hit its mid-2000s low in July 2005, more than two years before the recession began, and it fell to about 2.4% in June 2022 with no recession following ([Fisher Investments](https://www.fisherinvestments.com/en-us/insights/market-commentary/digging-into-americas-declining-savings-rate)). A New York Fed test found a one-point drop in the saving rate associated with only about a 0.024-point drop in real spending growth, and noted that asset booms mechanically depress the measure because capital gains are excluded from income while taxes on them are deducted ([NY Fed](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/current_issues/ci13-4.pdf)). The saving rate rises in recessions as a reaction; it does not lead them.

The distribution is less dire than the aggregate suggests, and more uneven. Bank of America reports deposits above inflation-adjusted 2019 levels in every income cohort, and lower-income after-tax wage growth of 4.7% ([BofA Institute](https://institute.bankofamerica.com/content/dam/economic-insights/consumer-checkpoint-november-2026.pdf)). Deposits of the bottom half of households by wealth are about 15% above end-2019 in real terms but about 16% below their 2021 peak (computed from [FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/WFRBLDEB50)). The fair statement for lower-income households is above 2019 in level, below trend, and far below the peak, with wage growth barely ahead of inflation. The wealth that supports spending sits elsewhere: the top 1% holds a record 32.5% of net worth ([24/7 Wall St](https://247wallst.com/personal-finance/2026/11/14/the-top-1-now-hold-32-5-of-u-s-household-wealth-the-highest-share-in-fed-records-going-back-to-1989-the-bottom-half-holds-2-3/)).

The saving rate becomes a warning when it stops being a lone signal. The evidence supports five conditions: the low reading persists after annual revision; real disposable income is flat or falling while real spending still grows for two quarters or more; unemployment starts to rise; lower-income liquid balances fall toward 2019 real levels; and debt service or delinquency flows rise. In November 2026 the second is close to met, with real disposable income flat in August and down 0.3% in September, and the fifth is partly met on delinquency but not on debt service. The other three are not met. One feature makes 2026 less forgiving than 2022: the Federal Reserve raised rates in September to 3.75% to 4.00%, two officials dissented in October in favor of another increase, and markets almost fully price a December hike ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20261028a.htm); [Reuters via Star-Advertiser](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/11/12/breaking-news/u-s-jobless-claims-rise-as-home-sales-hit-16-year-low/)). The benign exit of 2022, in which inflation fell and real incomes recovered, depends this time on energy prices retreating.

## Stress is narrow and deep: autos, FHA, student loans, the young and nonprime

The averages conceal a pattern in which a few segments are at or beyond post-2008 extremes while the largest pools of credit are sound. The New York Fed's Q3 2026 report captures both halves. The flow of debt into serious delinquency fell year on year for cards (6.87% from 7.05%), student loans (7.72% from 14.26%) and all debt combined (2.64% from 3.03%); it rose only for mortgages (1.60% from 1.28%) and autos (3.02% from 2.99%) ([NY Fed](https://www.newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2026/20261110)). Yet the share of all household debt in some stage of delinquency rose to about 5.0%, reported as the highest since early 2016 ([TheStreet Pro](https://pro.thestreet.com/market-commentary/household-debt-jumps-the-most-since-2022-delinquencies-climb-to-highest-since-2016)), because the stock of bad debt in the troubled segments keeps building.

**Autos are the one segment that is worse than the financial crisis on several measures.** The share of auto balances 90+ days past due reached 5.66% in Q3 2026, above the roughly 5% reached after 2008, and seriously delinquent auto balances rose about $15 billion in a year to $98 billion ([CU Collector, relaying NY Fed data](https://blog.cucollector.com/seriously-delinquent-auto-loan-debt-jumps-15-billion-as-borrower-distress-persists/)). Fitch's subprime 60+ day index set a record of 6.90% in January 2026 before easing to 6.13% in July under a revised methodology ([ESP Data](https://www.espdata.com/subprime-auto-loan-delinquencies-2026/)). The cause is loan structure as much as borrower income: 57.4% of loans written in August carried negative equity, loans longer than 72 months hit a record 31.3%, and the average new-loan payment passed $800 ([Cox Automotive](https://www.coxautoinc.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Q3-2026-Cox-Automotive-Sales-Forecast-Update-Transcript.pdf)). The direction in 2026 is mixed rather than uniformly worse. Cox reports subprime 60+ day delinquency a full point better year on year in August, while a different dataset of securitized subprime pools shows the highest September 30+ day rate since 2017, two-thirds of the rise coming from pool composition ([24/7 Wall St](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/11/02/auto-delinquencies-are-near-a-10-year-high-and-it-could-get-a-lot-worse/)). Auto is also where stress is creeping upward in credit quality: Fitch's prime 60-day index rose to 0.49% from 0.42%, still about half its Great Recession level ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/articles/repo-watch-good-credit-got-220000409.html)).

The mortgage deterioration is an FHA story. The Mortgage Bankers Association's Q2 2026 survey put FHA delinquency at 11.79% against 2.72% for conventional loans, with serious delinquency up 227 basis points year on year for FHA and 6 for conventional ([HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/mortgage-delinquency-q2-2026/)). TransUnion found FHA loans were 47.2% of mortgages 60+ days past due in Q3 2026 against 18.5% of originations ([United States Real Estate Investor](https://www.unitedstatesrealestateinvestor.com/transunion-q3-2026-fha-delinquency-47-percent/)). The New York Fed calls the overall mortgage rate "modest by historical standards"; the Q3 2009 peak in the serious-delinquency flow was 8.35%.

Student loans show the largest shock and a peaked inflow. The flow into serious delinquency halved, but 10.9% of balances are 90+ days past due and 9.3 million borrowers held $234 billion in default at June 2026 ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/adamminsky/2026/10/05/234-billion-in-student-loans-are-now-in-default-and-things-are-about-to-get-worse/)). The channel that matters for card and auto lenders is spillover: defaulted borrowers' average credit score fell 91 points to 476, and among them 56% were past due on credit cards and nearly 40% on auto loans, though they hold only about 2% of card balances ([Liberty Street Economics](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/05/federal-student-loan-defaults-return-after-pa)). The Fed's November Financial Stability Report, by contrast, describes student delinquency as not yet spilling into other debts ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/financial-stability-report-20261113.pdf)); the two statements are compatible only because the affected balances are small. Two unknowns remain: about 7 million former SAVE-plan borrowers are being moved into repayment, and whether wage garnishment has restarted could not be confirmed.

By age the picture is a barbell. In Q2 2026, card balances of borrowers aged 18 to 29 moved into serious delinquency at a 10.1% annual rate, close to 2010 levels, and the rate for those over 70 was the highest since 2011 ([24/7 Wall St](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/09/29/retirement-nightmare-serious-credit-card-delinquencies-for-seniors-hit-15-year-high/)). By income, the New York Fed finds card delinquency above pre-pandemic levels in every ZIP-code income quartile, with the gap between the lowest and highest quartiles widened to 5.93 points from 4.42 before the pandemic ([Liberty Street Economics](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/11/a-look-at-the-wider-divide-in-credit-card-delinquency-by-income/); [TheStreet Pro](https://pro.thestreet.com/market-commentary/household-debt-jumps-the-most-since-2022-delinquencies-climb-to-highest-since-2016)). Its explanation is partly reassuring: cards issued in 2020 to 2022 went to lower scores (median 688 against 699 before) and more often to low-income areas, that vintage peaked through 2023 and 2024, and the authors expect improvement to continue as it ages out, absent a macro shock.

The claim that the economy now runs on the top tenth of earners deserves caution. Moody's estimate that the top 10% account for more than 45% of spending is derived from income less estimated saving; the Consumer Expenditure Survey puts the figure at 23%, and the Minneapolis Fed concludes that "the available data do not align to tell a clear, K-shaped story" ([Minneapolis Fed](https://minneapolisfed.org/article/2026/have-us-consumers-gone-k-shaped-a-review-of-the-data)). Bank of America's October data show lower-income card spending growing fastest, at 5.9%, which more plausibly reflects the price of necessities than strength: Dollar General says its core customers "continue to be financially constrained" and are buying fewer items per trip, and McDonald's posted its first US same-store sales decline since early 2025 ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/economy/2026/dollar-stores-see-high-income-shoppers-hunting-value/); [AP via KPTV](https://www.kptv.com/2026/11/04/mcdonalds-us-sales-fall-more-customers-struggle-with-high-cost-living/?outputType=amp)).

Is stress moving up the ladder? There are early signs at the margins and no confirmation in the core. Middle- and higher-income shoppers are trading into dollar stores, utility arrears are growing fastest in near-prime tiers ([The Century Foundation](https://tcf.org/content/report/power-failure-rising-energy-debt-is-climbing-into-the-middle-class/)), and early-stage delinquency transitions for mortgages reached 3.97%, the highest since 2015. Against that, conventional mortgages, prime cards and aggregate flows are steady, and bankruptcy filings were up only 2% year on year in October ([ABI / Epiq](https://www.abi.org/node/1004659)). The accurate description is stress contained by loan type and credit tier, with behavior and non-credit arrears in the middle starting to turn first.

## Concern should rise when a labor signal joins the cash-flow signals

Two historical lessons frame the question of when to worry, and they point at different risks.

The first is that card losses on a 2008 scale require job losses. Card charge-offs ran at about 3.5% with unemployment at 4.7% in 2006 and peaked at 10.4% with unemployment at 9.9% ([PaymentsJournal / Mercator](https://www.paymentsjournal.com/unemployment-and-credit-losses-enough-to-force-change-in-credit-policy-through-2022/amp/)), which works out to roughly 1.3 points of charge-offs per point of unemployment. Applied to KPMG's bear case of 5.4% unemployment by end-2027 and about 6% in 2028 ([KPMG](https://kpmg.com/us/en/articles/2026/november-2026-economic-compass.html)), that slope implies industry card charge-offs of roughly 5.5% to 6.5% against 3.82% today. That is arithmetic on historical endpoints, not a sourced forecast. Research also finds that permanent layoffs, not temporary ones, drive card losses ([Liu and Liang](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/permanent-layoff-and-consumer-credit-card-loss-forecasting/)).

The second lesson is that losses can rise substantially without a recession. **Card charge-offs at all commercial banks went from 1.63% in Q4 2021 to 4.69% in Q3 2024 with no rise in unemployment** ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CORCCACBS)), driven by loose 2020 to 2022 underwriting and an inflation squeeze. In the same period the yield curve, two negative GDP quarters, falling temp employment and eventually the Sahm rule all gave false recession signals ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/12/23/economists-wrong-on-recession-northern-trust-ryan-boyle-3-culprits/)). For an investor in consumer lenders this is the more relevant precedent, because its ingredients are present again: need-driven borrowing (banks told the Fed that card demand strengthened because of "customers' spending needs in response to price changes") alongside a renewed rise in lower-score originations ([Federal Reserve SLOOS](https://www.federalreserve.gov/data/sloos/sloos-202610.htm)).

A third lesson concerns which headline to distrust. In 2007, 30+ day card delinquency was falling and at its lowest since 2003 while charge-offs and bankruptcies rose ([American Banker](https://www.americanbanker.com/payments/news/charge-offs-are-on-the-rise-is-the-worst-yet-to-come)). Balance-weighted delinquency is a comfortable number late in a cycle. The modern equivalent is the TransUnion divergence between balance-level and borrower-level delinquency described above.

On the evidence, signals would appear in roughly this order. Real income and the saving rate deteriorate and households' own expectations of missing payments rise; this is already happening, with the New York Fed survey's probability of missing a minimum payment jumping 1.6 points to 13.8% in October and expectations of higher unemployment at their highest since April 2020 ([NY Fed SCE](https://newyorkfed.org/newsevents/news/research/2026/20261109)). Next, early-delinquency flows rise in autos and then cards, and borrower-count delinquency diverges from balance-weighted delinquency; autos and the borrower-count divergence are already there, card flows are not. Then lenders tighten terms before headline standards; limits and score cut-offs have tightened while headline standards are unchanged. Then temp employment, hours and quits fall and jobless claims rise; none of this has happened, with initial claims at 200,000 and announced layoffs down 71% year on year in October ([Reuters via Star-Advertiser](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/11/12/breaking-news/u-s-jobless-claims-rise-as-home-sales-hit-16-year-low/); [Challenger via CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/?p=193395)). Unemployment and the Sahm rule come last, by which point charge-offs are already rising. Issuer reserve releases and marketing increases are lagging and pro-cyclical; they show that lenders see nothing in their own books yet, not that nothing is coming.

## Conclusion

The initial reading mistook two measurement artifacts for a diagnosis. The small-bank series is a portrait of a few specialist lenders, and the saving rate is a revisable residual that reacts to shocks instead of predicting them. Corrected, the evidence describes a consumer who is employed, squeezed on real income, and financing the gap from saving, with credit damage confined to products and borrowers that were weakest going in. That is a more fragile position than the issuer numbers alone suggest, because what is holding it together is employment and asset prices, and the Federal Reserve is tightening into it instead of cushioning it.

For someone who follows consumer lenders, the practical implication is that there are two distinct risks with different clocks. The recession risk is visible in advance through labor data and is not showing. The quieter risk is a repeat of 2022 to 2024 in smaller form: issuers reopened to lower-score borrowers in 2026 on the strength of loss rates that their own managements attribute largely to mix, at the moment households began borrowing to cover necessities. That would surface first as year-on-year increases in the monthly delinquency filings from Synchrony, Capital One and Bread, and in borrower-count delinquency, some quarters before it reaches reported charge-offs. As of November 2026 those filings still show improvement. Nothing in this research measures Nu Holdings' Latin American book, which needs its own evidence.
